#### The clear passes on durability — *sourced lines and tables only; verdict prose dropped*
**Hermès — the control, and it behaved as a control should.** Operating margin 40.5% → 42.1% → 40.5% → 41.0% → 41.0%: a 110bps range across the worst luxury downturn in fifteen years, with gross margin *rising* 40bps in H1 2026 to 71.1% on what the company calls "an exceptional sell-through rate" `[sourced]` — i.e. no discounting. Leather Goods grew +9.8% at constant FX in a half when the group's Asia-ex-Japan grew +2.4% `[sourced]`: the constraint is artisan capacity, not demand. CFO du Halgouët: *"we remain true to our principle of passing through our input costs as little as possible"* `[commentary]`. This is pricing power exercised through restraint. At 21.4x EV/EBIT it will not pass a cheapness gate, and it was never expected to — its job here was to calibrate what a passing grade looks like.
**Moncler — the finding of the sweep.** The only name whose gross margin did not fall at all: 77.1% → 78.1% → 78.1%, and 77.2% in H1 2026 against 76.9%. EBIT margin eroded 80bps across the whole trough. No clearance or markdown language appears anywhere in its FY2025 or H1 2026 disclosures `[sourced absence]`. The dividend was raised to €1.40 while net cash built to €1,458m. And management states the composition of growth outright — Luciano Santel, H1 2026 call: ***"Pricing was predominant. Volumes were flattish, slightly negative in the second quarter."*** `[commentary]` That is the textbook signature: take price, hold units.
**Three honest qualifications on Moncler.** EMEA comparable performance is negative (−4% cFX in H1, −8% in Q2), attributed to tourist flows `[commentary]`; growth is increasingly Asia-weighted (Asia +19% cFX in H1); lease liabilities of €1,198.6m now exceed net cash of €1,112.4m at 30 June 2026 `[sourced]`, so the "net cash" framing needs care. And the group figure blends two different businesses — Stone Island grew +11% cFX *without* meaningful price increases ("about low single digit") `[commentary]`, i.e. on volume. The pricing-power evidence belongs to the Moncler brand, which is 84.5% of revenue `[computed]`.

#### The absorbers — *sourced lines and tables only; verdict prose dropped*
**Richemont — strongest constant-currency evidence in the set, and I could not price it.** Group gross margin fell 430bps to 64.4% and operating margin 90bps to 20.0%, which looks like erosion. The decomposition says otherwise and is fully disclosed: two-thirds of the production-cost increase was **gold**, one-third was **~€200m of US duties**, and FX cost a further 210bps `[sourced]`. At constant rates, Jewellery Maisons' operating margin **rose 150bps** and Specialist Watchmakers' **rose 240bps** `[sourced]`. Richemont is also the only company here whose management attributes growth to price *and* volume — Bos describes *"limited yet real price increases"* of roughly 5–6% alongside volume and mix `[commentary]`. Q1 FY2027 confirms the demand side emphatically: group **+20% at constant rates**, Jewellery **+24%**, Retail **+24%**, Americas +27%, Japan +36% `[sourced]`.
The caveat is that this is two companies in one. Specialist Watchmakers' operating margin went 19.0% → 3.4% in three years `[sourced]` — that segment has pricing power in name only. **Richemont was left unpriced above because converting a CHF market cap against a EUR profit stream needs a dated FX rate I could not source, and the framework forbids inventing one.** That is a thirty-second fix on a PC session and it should be done, because on the durability evidence Richemont belongs in the ANALYSE queue alongside Moncler and LVMH.

#### The lost-price group — *sourced lines and tables only; verdict prose dropped*
**Kering — the counter-example that defines the category.** Gucci revenue fell from €10,487m (FY2022) to €5,992m (FY2025), **−43%**; recurring operating income from €3,732m to €966m, **−74%**; margin from 35.6% to 16.1% `[sourced]`. Group margin fell 1,640bps. The dividend went €14.00 → €4.00, a **71% cut**. And CEO Luca de Meo's own diagnosis is explicit: past repricing ***"had a very big impact on the volume. The elasticity was not exactly linear, it was exponential."*** `[commentary]` That is a management admission that Gucci raised prices without the brand equity to support them — the precise inverse of pricing power.
The H1 2026 stabilisation is real and should be credited: group comparable +1% after twelve quarters of decline, Gucci margin +100bps to 17.0%, Gucci Q2 retail −2% comparable against −9% (a 7-point sequential improvement), net debt down €4.7bn to €3.3bn `[sourced]`. But the improvement came from **OpEx down 5% and reduced off-price activity**, not from price realisation `[sourced]`. At **20.8x EV/trough EBIT**, Kering is a turnaround bet on brand repair, not a value-investing entry on durable economics. **Pass on this framework.**
**Swatch — cannot be assessed, and that is the finding.** Swatch **discloses no gross margin line in any year** `[sourced absence]`, which removes the single most diagnostic metric. Operating margin fell 15.1% → 4.5% → 2.1% → 1.7%, but the collapse is substantially not a pricing phenomenon: a ~30% fall in China/HK/Macau took the region from 33% to 27% of sales; management deliberately held Swiss production capacity and headcount (*"We can also accept to have less profits. But we stick with our people"* `[commentary]`), driving severe under-absorption; and FX cost roughly CHF 308m in FY2025 `[sourced]`. The direct pricing evidence is actually good — Hayek raised US prices **5–15%** after tariffs and reported *"Americans are continuing to buy"* `[commentary]`; the US then grew **+27%** in H1 2026 `[sourced]`.
**Burberry — pricing power demonstrably lost, credibly being regained.** The FY25 gross margin of 62.5%, down 810bps from the FY23 peak, was in the company's own words *"primarily driven by inventory clearance actions"* `[sourced]` — the definition of a brand that had to discount. Adjusted operating margin went to 1.0%, the reported line to a £3m loss, and **the dividend was suspended entirely** — no other name here went to zero. The recovery is real: FY26 gross margin **+530bps at constant currency to 67.9%**, comparable sales +2%, and Q1 FY27 delivered a **fourth consecutive quarter of positive comps at +5%** with Americas +12% and Greater China +9% `[sourced]`. CFO Ferry ties it to price, not just cost: *"growth in full price and lower markdown year-on-year"* `[commentary]`.
**Hugo Boss — price discipline, not pricing power, and the distinction matters.** Gross margin sat in a 61.5–61.8% band for five straight years and then **expanded 160bps to 63.7% in H1 2026 (200bps in Q2) while sales fell 8%** `[sourced]`. That combination only occurs when a company chooses price over volume, and management says so: *"prioritizing full price sell-through"*; *"deliberately sharpening our wholesale distribution by reducing long-tail exposure… prioritize full price quality over volume"* `[commentary]`. Real discipline.
It is not the same as pricing power. FY2026 guidance concedes a mid-to-high-single-digit sales decline and EBIT of €300–350m against €391m `[sourced]` — price is being defended by shrinking the business. The **dividend was cut 97% to the €0.04 legal minimum** `[sourced]`, with capital redirected to a €200m buyback. Only BOSS Menswear grew (+3% cFX); HUGO fell 4%, BOSS Womenswear 5%. And Grieder's own framing — *"We haven't done just price increases for the sake of price increases"* `[commentary]` — concedes that pure price was not available.

### Ranking — evidence of pricing power through the trough
This ranks durability evidence only, not cheapness.

1. **Hermès** — unambiguous. 110bps of total margin variance, gross margin expanding on sell-through, supply-constrained growth in the weakest region, price held deliberately below cost pass-through.
2. **Moncler** — gross margin flat-to-up while every peer's fell; management confirms growth was price-led with flat volumes; no clearance; dividend raised.
3. **Richemont** — constant-currency divisional margins *expanded* at both Jewellery (+150bps) and Watches (+240bps) while absorbing a gold shock, €200m of duties and a 210bps FX hit; the only group claiming price *and* volume. Marked down only because Specialist Watchmakers at a 3.4% margin shows one franchise without pricing power.
4. **LVMH** — margin defended at 22.5% and gross margin up, but six consecutive years of FLG margin decline totalling 650bps and a CFO statement that volume/mix is flat. Holding the line, not widening it.
5. **Hugo Boss** — price discipline verified in the gross margin, but delivered by contracting the business.
6. **Swatch** — good direct pricing evidence, unreadable margin signal, no gross-margin disclosure, CHF 7.3bn inventory overhang.
7. **Burberry** — lost it (clearance-driven 810bps gross margin collapse, dividend suspended), now regaining it. Measures *recovered* pricing power, not power *held*.
8. **Kering** — lost it, with the CEO's "exponential elasticity" remark settling the question.

**The single sharpest discriminator in the dataset:** at the trough, Hermès and Richemont grew on volume *and* price; LVMH grew on price alone with flat volume; Kering discovered that raising price destroyed volume faster than it added revenue.

### Two caveats that cut in Richemont's favour, and why they do not change the verdict — *sourced lines and tables only; verdict prose dropped*

It is also worth noting who else likes it: Bernstein named Richemont, not LVMH, its top sector pick on 2026-09-03 `[sourced]`. That is market context, not a valuation input — but a name that is both the consensus pick and the most expensive in its peer set is exactly where this framework should be most sceptical.

### 4. Process finding — three secondary sources returned wrong figures this run, and two were caught only by luck
**This is the most transferable output of the session and it should be adopted as a standing rule.**

| # | What was fetched | What came back | How it was caught |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | A MarketScreener quote URL with a guessed numeric ID for Royal Unibrew | **Nedbank Group, ZAR 298.81, stamped 2026-09-04** | The currency was wrong. Had the wrong company been another DKK-quoted name, it would not have been. |
| 2 | A MarketScreener quote URL with a guessed numeric ID for Carlsberg | **Per Aarsleff Holding, DKK 721.00, stamped "04/09/2026 BST"** | **Only because the extraction was asked to confirm the company name.** Right currency, right exchange, right date stamp, plausible magnitude for Carlsberg. **This would have entered the register undetected.** |
| 3 | A GlobeNewswire resource-download URL surfaced by a search for Carlsberg's H1 2026 report | **Scandinavian Tobacco Group's interim report** — with a NIBD/EBITDA of "3.0x", the same figure Carlsberg reports | Only because the extraction was asked to identify the document. **The matching 3.0x would have corroborated a wrong balance sheet.** |
| 4 | An earnings-call summary for Carlsberg | Britvic synergy target as **"DKK 110 million"**; the annual report says **GBP 110m** | The primary document was in hand. |
| 5 | The same summary | Year-end 2025 leverage as **"3.9x"**; the annual report says **3.28x** | Same. |

**Two standing rules, proposed for adoption:**

- **Never construct a provider URL from a guessed numeric ID.** Resolve it by search every time, and **require every fetched page or document to state the company name and period before any number is taken from it.** Items 2 and 3 both passed every sanity check the register currently applies — right currency, right date, plausible magnitude — and were caught only by the identity question.
- **A secondary source may corroborate a primary one; it may never substitute for one.** Items 4 and 5 were both wrong in ways that mattered (a currency and a leverage ratio), and both came from the same summary of the same call.

**This also partially rehabilitates the five `u` rows.** The register's existing caution about unconfirmed prices is correct, but the failure mode it guards against is *absence* of a stamp. **The more dangerous failure is a confident, well-formed, correctly-stamped price for the wrong company**, and nothing in the current schema catches it. **Add the company name as returned by the provider to the price-verification step.**

#### The two notes the table needs — *sourced lines and tables only; verdict prose dropped*
**Jungheinrich's 71.8x is not a valuation, it is a broken denominator.** Trailing EPS of €0.37 against a €26.62 share price reflects an earnings collapse, not a rich multiple `[sourced]`. Separately, the €1.34bn market capitalisation printed for the listed preference line does not represent the whole company — Jungheinrich has two share classes and only one is listed. **Nothing in this row is usable without the full capital structure**, and it was not obtained. Deferred, not dismissed.

## Japan governance / capital-return reform — evidence base (JPX disclosure and PBR/ROE statistics)

### Verdict — the queue's premise is wrong, and it should be corrected rather than carried forward — *sourced lines and tables only; verdict prose dropped*
Two findings drive that, both `[sourced]`.
**First, the theme's own arithmetic says the broad trade is largely finished at the end where disclosure is adequate.** Among Prime Market companies above USD 1bn, the share trading below 1x price-to-book has fallen from **43% in July 2022 to roughly 14% (109 companies) by March 2026** `[sourced, ION Analytics/Mergermarket, 2026-03]`. Across the whole Prime Market the fall is 50% → **27%** `[sourced, JPX, data at end-February 2026]`. A screen whose population has shrunk by two-thirds in under four years is not an undiscovered hunting ground; it is a crowded one being harvested.
**Second, and more fundamental: the reform theme selects on the wrong variable.** A sub-1x book multiple is, in the great majority of cases, the market correctly pricing a business that earns less than its cost of capital. JPX's own data makes this explicit — **43% of Prime and 60% of Standard companies still earn ROE below 8%, improvements of only 4 and 3 percentage points respectively since July 2022** `[sourced, JPX]`. Set that against the 23-point collapse in the sub-1x PBR population and the conclusion is hard to avoid: **the multiple has re-rated roughly six times faster than the underlying returns have improved.** `[computed]`

#### Disclosure penetration — the programme has largely done its job in Prime, and stalled in Standard — *sourced lines and tables only; verdict prose dropped*

|---|---|---|

**That divergence is the theme in one line: bigger cheques from fewer companies.** `[inference]` The marginal company is no longer joining in. For an investor arriving in late 2026, the implication is that the easy population-level effect is spent and what remains is name-specific.

### Strongest honest counter-argument to this verdict — *sourced lines and tables only; verdict prose dropped*
The bear case on my own conclusion deserves stating, because it is not weak. Reform of this kind works with long lags: disclosure precedes behaviour, behaviour precedes returns, and four years is early to conclude that ROE will not follow PBR. The **71% of Prime companies that have *updated* their disclosures** `[sourced]` is evidence of engagement rather than one-off box-ticking, and cross-shareholding unwinds release capital slowly by construction. It is entirely possible that the 2022–26 period re-rated the multiple and the 2026–30 period improves the returns, in which case a framework that steps away now steps away at exactly the wrong time.
