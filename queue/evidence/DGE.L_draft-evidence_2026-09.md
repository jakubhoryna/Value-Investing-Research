### Gate 1 — Do I understand it?
**Yes on the business. The accounts require more work than any other name in the bucket.**

Diageo owns Johnnie Walker, Smirnoff, Captain Morgan, Tanqueray, Baileys, Don Julio, Casamigos, Guinness and a large Chinese white-spirits business, sold across North America, Europe, Asia Pacific, Latin America & Caribbean and Africa.

**The accounting problem is the size of the gap between adjusted and reported.** `[computed on sourced figures]`

| Reported operating profit | 3,156 |

**That is 12.9% of net sales excluded from the headline profit measure in a single year** `[computed]`, and it broadly reconciles to two disclosed items: **$1.5bn of impairment charges** — largely **Türkiye**, driven by hyperinflationary accounting and a pricing-strategy change, plus a **$287m write-down of the Don Papa rum brand** — and **$0.9bn of restructuring charges**, of which **~$752m relates to implementing a new operating framework** `[sourced]`.

**Neither is a non-event.** Restructuring is cash. Impairment is the admission that assets were bought for more than they are worth. **A framework that values Diageo on the 28.9% adjusted margin and ignores a $2.5bn annual gap is valuing a company that does not exist.** `[inference]`

### The answer, first — *sourced lines and tables only; verdict prose dropped*
**The structural threat to US spirits is not a volume threat. It is a revenue-per-case threat.** In calendar 2025, US spirits **volume rose 1.9%** while **supplier revenue fell 2.2%** `[sourced — DISCUS, briefing 2026-02-05]`. Americans bought **more** cases of spirits and paid **less** for them.
**And it reframes Pernod Ricard's number.** Pernod's US net sales fell **14% organically** in FY2026 `[sourced]` against a US spirits market whose supplier revenue fell **2.2%** in calendar 2025 `[sourced]`. **The periods do not match — DISCUS is calendar 2025, Pernod's year ends June 2026 — so the gap cannot be read as a precise share loss.** But a roughly twelve-point gap is far too large to be timing alone. **Most of Pernod's US decline is company-specific — destocking, portfolio mix, share — not the market.** `[inference]` The market is a slow structural headwind; the −14% is not the market.

### 1. What the US data actually says — *sourced lines and tables only; verdict prose dropped*
`[sourced — Distilled Spirits Council of the United States, annual economic briefing, 2026-02-05, covering calendar 2025]`
| | 2025 | Change |
|---|---|---|
| **US spirits supplier revenue** | **US$36.4bn** | **−2.2%** |
| **US spirits volume** | **318.1m nine-litre cases** | **+1.9%** |
| Spirits' share of total US beverage alcohol | 42.4% | share lead maintained |
| Category | Revenue | Rev. change | Volume | Vol. change |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Vodka | $7.0bn | −3.0% | 72.5m cases | −2.2% |
| Tequila / mezcal | $6.4bn | **−4.1%** | 32.1m cases | **−0.3%** |

**Tequila is the cleanest single illustration:** volume essentially flat at **−0.3%**, revenue down **−4.1%** `[sourced]`. **Nobody stopped drinking tequila. They stopped buying the expensive one.**

### 2. The generational thesis is not supported by the data — *sourced lines and tables only; verdict prose dropped*
**The "Gen Z rejects alcohol" narrative, which underpins most of the structural bear case in the press, is contradicted by the industry's own tracking data.** `[sourced — IWSR Bevtrac, reported 2026-07]`
- A second source records the same reversal: **66% of legal-drinking-age Gen Z drank in March 2023, rising to 73% by March 2025** `[sourced — IWSR, via FoodNavigator 2026-02-13]`.
**Gen Z's low participation was a 2020–2023 phenomenon that has substantially normalised, and the evidence points to affordability rather than values as its cause** `[sourced — Euromonitor commentary]`. **A framework that has been discounting spirits terminal values on generational rejection has been discounting them for a reason the data does not support.** `[inference]`
**But moderation is real, and IWSR calls it structural in terms the framework should take seriously.** Drinkers across *all* generations report drinking **less frequently and less per occasion**, with average consumption falling to **3.9 drinks per occasion from 4.4** — an **11% decline** `[computed on sourced figures]`. IWSR's president: the trend *"increasingly appears to be driven by lifestyle choices, resulting in a structural, rather than cyclical, change"* `[sourced, verbatim]`.
**So: participation is stable, intensity is falling.** That is a volume headwind of a specific size — global alcohol volume declined at a **−1% CAGR from 2019 to 2024**, with the **US at −1%** and China at −3% `[sourced — IWSR]`.

### 3. GLP-1 — the largest genuine uncertainty, and it should be sized rather than asserted — *sourced lines and tables only; verdict prose dropped*
**Prevalence is now material and rising fast** `[sourced — Gallup, survey 28 May – 5 June 2026, n=5,065 US adults, ±1.5pp]`:
**Effect on drinking, where the estimates are much softer** `[sourced — Morgan Stanley research, via FoodNavigator]`: GLP-1s can reduce alcohol consumption *"by as much as 75%, and 50% per occasion."* Euromonitor's caution is recorded alongside it: *"it's difficult to measure the exact effect of GLP-1s on the alcohol market."*
| Assumed reduction among users | Population-level volume effect at 11% penetration |
|---|---|
| 30% | **−3.3%** |
| 50% (the per-occasion figure) | **−5.5%** |
| 75% (the upper bound quoted) | **−8.3%** |
**The check that matters: US spirits volume grew 1.9% in 2025** `[sourced]`. **Whatever GLP-1s are doing to alcohol, it is not currently visible as an aggregate volume decline in the largest spirits market.** Either the effect is smaller than the arithmetic implies, or it is offsetting growth that would otherwise be visible, or it lags. **All three are possible and the data cannot separate them.** `[inference]`
