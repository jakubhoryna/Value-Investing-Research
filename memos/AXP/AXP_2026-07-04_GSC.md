*Documented substitution — the mechanical ROCE screens cannot characterise a deposit-funded lender (Appendix C).

A superb franchise wrapped around two disqualifying facts. The franchise: a 176-year premium brand and closed-loop network compounding EPS at ~12%/yr on ~29% average ROE with best-in-class credit. The disqualifiers: the single largest input — the Delta co-brand and rewards relationship — cost ≈ $8.2bn in 2025 (Delta's disclosure), ~15% of AXP's total expenses, a hard supplier-breadth fail with no ready alternative and escalating terms at every renewal; and CEO compensation of $46.2m in 2025, half again over the $30m strike. Both gates fail independently — AVOID on quality and governance, not price — and at 14.8× FCF (~22× earnings) it is EXPENSIVE anyway. A flip needs comp reform *and* a diversified partner cost base; neither is in prospect before the 2029 Delta renewal.

## 2. Understanding

American Express is a 176-year-old, New York-headquartered, NYSE-listed bank holding company running an integrated, closed-loop payments franchise: it issues premium fee-based charge and credit cards to affluent consumers and businesses (who pay card fees and interest), acquires and serves the merchants who pay discount fees on that spend, and operates the network in between, funding a ~$220bn receivables-and-loan book chiefly with $152bn of deposits, earning most of its revenue in the US as one of the world's most prominent premium payments brands. **PASS.**

## 3. Quality — past record

The book's CE screens are structurally inapplicable — earning assets sit in capital employed while their deposit funding cannot leave it, so even the most generous read caps normalized 10y ROCE at 5.4–7.2% (worst-of-four 5.4%): a literal WEAK that describes the accounting, not the economics (Appendix C). On the documented lender substitution the record is emphatic: 10y average ROE 28.6% (worst year 13.6%, COVID-2020); EPS geo-mean +11.9%, FCF/sh +13.4%, BV/sh +9.1%, TBV/sh +9.6% with a 26% share-count reduction — all far above the 3% floor, no sustained decline. Non-deposit interest-bearing debt/equity 173%, inside the 200% flag; total L/E 796% is deposit funding. **STRONG** (substituted; final verdict unchanged under the literal alternate).

## 4. Quality — future

Breadth: customers PASS (tens of millions of card members, millions of merchants; none >10%). Suppliers **FAIL** — Delta received ≈ $8.2bn from AXP in 2025 (~15% of $53.2bn total expenses), rising 11%/yr toward a stated $10bn; the ready-alternatives exception is unavailable — rival airlines are contracted to competing issuers and the SkyMiles currency behind 13% of billed business and 21% of Card Member loans is not substitutable. Forces: customer power WEAK, supplier power STRONG (partners extract escalating remuneration at each renewal), substitutes WEAK, new entrants WEAK — tally 1 strong. Moat: Brand — premium positioning behind the industry's highest card fees and discount rates, closed-loop reinforced — stable. Market growth YES. Breadth FAIL decides: **WEAK.**

## 5. Shareholder-friendliness

## 7. Risks & thesis-breakers

- Delta renewal (agreement ends 2029): each renegotiation has transferred more economics to the partner; losing it would be worse.
- Premium-card arms race (JPMorgan Sapphire et al.) inflates rewards/services cost — Card Member services grew 27% in 2025.
- Credit cycle: the loan book roughly doubled since 2016; 2020 showed earnings halving in a shock.
- Regulatory: discount-rate scrutiny; bank-capital rules constrain distributions.
- Governance: a board comfortable with $46m CEO pay is the book's window into decision quality.
