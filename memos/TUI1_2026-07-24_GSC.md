TUI is the cleanest example this register has produced of a statistically cheap business that the quality gates reject. At €6.662 both primaries pass — EV/OI 6.4× on the conservative EV and 4.1× with cash credited, MCap/FCF 3.4× — and MCap/BV 1.9× clears its qualifier. None of that survives contact with the record. Across FY2016–FY2025 the worst of the four normalised ROCE averages is 2.9% and FCFROCE 1.8%, against ADEQUATE lines of 10% and a STRONG line of 8%. Operating income per share compounded at −18.6% a year and book value per share at −25.7%, because the equity base was rebuilt from ~583m shares to 558m post-consolidation (≈5.6bn pre-consolidation equivalents) through pandemic rescue financing. Liabilities are 877% of book equity, past P6's 700% ceiling and without any of the conditions that would excuse it. Nothing about the price flips this: the framework's answer to a cheap bad business is that it is a bad business. What would need to change is not the multiple but the ROCE, the leverage, and the share count — a multi-year repair, not a re-rating.

## 2. Understanding

TUI AG is a Hanover-headquartered, XETRA-listed vertically integrated tourism group that sells package holidays, flights, hotel stays, cruises and destination experiences to European consumers — principally in the UK, Germany, the Nordics, Benelux and Central Europe — through its own tour-operator brands, retail agencies and digital channels, and supplies much of that holiday itself via 463 owned, joint-ventured and managed hotels, five airlines, three cruise businesses (Marella wholly owned; TUI Cruises and Hapag-Lloyd through a 50% joint venture with Royal Caribbean) and the TUI Musement excursions arm. **PASS.**

## 3. Quality — past record

Worst-of-four normalised ROCE 2.9% (range 2.9–3.7%), FCFROCE 1.8% (1.8–2.2%) — both far below P3. The distribution matters as much as the average: FY2016–FY2019 delivered ROCE roughly 8–10%, FY2020–FY2021 destroyed €4.9bn of cumulative operating income, and FY2022–FY2025 recovered to €1,362m of EBIT on a larger capital base — a genuine recovery, but it does not repair a ten-year record. Per-share growth is catastrophic on every measure (OI/sh −18.6%, FCF/sh −9.1%, BV/sh −25.7% geometric). L/E is 877%, breaching both P5 and P6. **WEAK** — four independent failures, any one of which is sufficient.

## 4. Quality — future

Breadth **PASS** on both legs: ~20m individual holidaymakers with no customer near a tenth of revenue, and a fragmented hotel and airline supply base with ready alternatives (the Boeing/Airbus duopoly touches capex, not COGS). Forces: customer bargaining power **STRONG** (price-transparent, zero switching cost, trivially comparison-shopped); supplier power **WEAK** (TUI is a scale buyer of fragmented bed stock); substitutes **STRONG** (self-assembled travel via OTAs, Booking, Airbnb; and doing-without, since a holiday is the most deferrable large purchase a household makes); new entrants **WEAK** (fleet, licensing, ATOL-style bonding and working-capital demands are real barriers). Tally 2 strong — P16's stop signal. Moat: brand plus owned-capacity scale, **stable but not pricing-powerful**; it has not defended margin. Market growth **MIXED** — volumes recovered, but the category is cyclical and structurally low-single-digit. **WEAK.**

## 5. Shareholder-friendliness

Compensation is clean: the highest-paid Executive Board member (Ebel) took €2.70m granted-and-owed against a 5%-of-lower(FCF €989.6m, NI €635.9m) line of €31.8m, and nowhere near the ~$40m CPI-indexed scrutiny trigger; the Supervisory Board chair's fixed fee is €270k, below German large-cap norms and far under any 2× stop. No buybacks anywhere in the window — the company was issuing, not retiring — so P19's repurchase tests do not engage, and there is no acquisition above the 15×/20× lines. Related-party dealings are €469.6m received, overwhelmingly hotel services bought from the Riu and TUI Cruises joint ventures: 2.0% of the relevant expense line, disclosed, and confirmed by the Audit Committee. One flag logged, not two: the FY2020–FY2023 capital-allocation record, in which pre-COVID payout was followed by dilution that permanently transferred value away from incumbent holders. **PASS.**

## 7. Risks & thesis-breakers

- The recovery is real and continuing — FY2025 EBIT €1,362m was the best of the decade — so an investor who ignores the ten-year record can construct a plausible bull case that the framework deliberately refuses.
- Leverage of 877% leaves almost no cushion: a single bad summer, a fuel or FX shock, or a geopolitical closure of a major destination lands directly on a thin equity base.
- Package holidays are the most cyclically exposed consumer category in Europe; the next demand shock repeats FY2020 arithmetic on a still-levered balance sheet.
- Structural substitution by self-assembled travel continues to erode the tour operator's reason to exist, and TUI's own digital pivot competes against better-capitalised platforms.
- Cheapness itself is a trap here: at 3.4× FCF the market is pricing a genuine possibility of value destruction, not an oversight.

## 8. Flip conditions & next review

To WATCH: the window must roll far enough that FY2020–FY2021 leave it (FY2031 at the earliest on a pure ten-year basis) **and** worst-of-four ROCE must reach 10%+ **and** L/E must fall back inside P5's 200%, **and** the forces tally must fall below two strong — the last of which requires a structural change in how Europeans buy holidays, not better execution. Any one of those alone is insufficient. To a harder stop: a renewed demand shock that pushes equity negative again. Next review is the FY2026 annual report (~Dec 2026), informational only — the arithmetic cannot flip before then.

## C. Calculation notes

**Operating income definition.** TUI's income statement has no "operating profit" line. OI is taken as TUI's own reported EBIT = EBT − financial income + financial expenses, which is arithmetically identical to Revenue − cost of sales − administrative expenses + other income − other expenses − impairments + share of result of equity-accounted investments. Verified line-by-line for every year (FY2025: 1,034.2 − 101.0 + 429.0 = 1,362.2, and the direct build gives 1,362.2). The JV/associate result is **included** because TUI Cruises and the Riu hotel vehicles are core operations and their carrying value (€1,716.5m at FY2025) sits inside capital employed — numerator and denominator kept consistent, the same principle §2·1A applies to captive-finance arms. Excluding it would understate returns by roughly 4 percentage points of ROCE in recent years and would not change any verdict.

**FY2025 CE build (€m).** Total assets 18,148.9 − NIBCL 7,597.5 = **CE_wc 10,551.4**; less cash & equivalents 3,120.2 and current other financial assets 12.1 = **CE_xc 7,419.1**.
NIBCL = trade payables 3,355.4 + touristic advance payments received 4,094.3 + current income tax liabilities 147.8 = 7,597.5.
**NIBCL burden-of-proof calls.** Touristic advance payments received are customer prepayments for holidays not yet taken — deferred revenue in substance, and TUI itself classifies them as contract liabilities — so they are deducted. Current "other non-financial liabilities" (€657.7m), current "other provisions" (€512.9m), derivative liabilities and current lease liabilities are **left in CE**: they are ambiguous or financing in nature, and the rule puts the burden on the item. Short-term financial liabilities and the current portion of lease liabilities always stay in CE.

**FY2025 TBV build.** BV (equity excluding NCI) 1,762.5 − goodwill 2,933.6 − other intangibles 596.8 = **−1,767.9**. Cross-checked against the independent stockanalysis figure of −1,767.9 for FY2025 and matched exactly for FY2021–FY2024, confirming the method.

**FY2025 EV build.** MCap 6.662 × 507.431m = 3,380.5. Debt = non-current financial liabilities 1,562.2 + current 420.6 + non-current lease liabilities 1,768.7 + current lease liabilities 685.8 = 4,437.3 (lease liabilities treated as debt-equivalent per §2·1A). Preferred nil. NCI 924.2.
- **Conservative (no cash excess):** 3,380.5 + 4,437.3 + 924.2 = **8,742.0**
- **All-cash-excess:** 8,742.0 − 3,132.3 = **5,609.7**

**Goodwill treatment.** Retained in CE. TUI is not a serial acquirer today, but the €2.93bn balance is the legacy of the 2014 TUI AG / TUI Travel plc combination and is still supported by the businesses in the register; deducting it would flatter an already-failing return series.

**IFRS interest reclassification (mandatory).** TUI classifies interest paid within financing. Interest paid has been moved into CFO before deriving FCF for every year: 2016 92.3 · 2017 74.8 · 2018 110.8 · 2019 117.9 · 2020 251.9 · 2021 404.8 · 2022 385.6 · 2023 435.6 · 2024 384.7 · 2025 329.7. Capex = payments for investments in property, plant and equipment and intangible assets; payments for investments in other non-current assets (financial in nature) are excluded, logged.

**P18 lease capitalisation (pre-IFRS 16 years).** TUI adopted IFRS 16 in FY2020, so FY2016–FY2019 are pre-adoption. Trigger test (undiscounted operating-lease commitments ÷ total assets): FY2017 19.6%, FY2018 18.0%, FY2019 16.9% — all above the 10% line, so capitalisation is required. Discount rate 4.99%, TUI's own disclosed weighted-average incremental borrowing rate at the IFRS 16 transition date. FY2019 uses the company's disclosed transition present value of €2,368.6m directly. FY2017 (€2,328.0m) and FY2018 (€2,352.1m) are PVs computed from the disclosed maturity buckets, allocating the 1–5 year lump evenly across years 2–5, the 5–10 year lump across years 6–10, and the beyond-ten-years lump across years 11–15 along the visible downtrend. Effects applied: lease PV added to CE (both variants) and to the L/E numerator; OI increased by imputed interest (rate × PV); FCF increased by lease payments less imputed interest. FY2016 is unadjusted — see gap 1 in Appendix A.

**P20 not active.** Capex/sales was 3.1% in FY2025 against 4.9% pre-COVID (FY2018), i.e. below the pre-cycle ratio rather than far above it, so P20's first condition fails outright. FY2025 order commitments of €1,952.1m (€993.5m due FY2026) reflect fleet renewal at Marella and 737 MAX deliveries but do not constitute a documented capex super-cycle on the framework's test. MCap/FCF therefore stands as a live primary.

**Share-count basis.** TUI executed a 10:1 share consolidation in 2023. FY2016–FY2020 weighted-average counts derived from reported earnings attributable to TUI AG shareholders ÷ reported EPS, then divided by 10 to state them on the current basis (FY2016 582.8m → 58.28m, and so on). FY2016–FY2020 EPS were reported with basic equal to diluted. FY2021–FY2025 counts are as reported on the post-consolidation basis; FY2025 diluted (558.16m) exceeds shares outstanding (507.43m) because of the 2021 and 2024 convertible bonds. Market capitalisation uses shares outstanding; the trigger band uses fully diluted shares per §5. Note: one aggregated source shows 503.2m shares outstanding on a trailing basis against 507.4m at the FY2025 balance-sheet date; no repurchase appears in the FY2025 cash flow statement, and the difference is treated as a data artifact, flagged for the next REVIEW.

**Bias check: none live.** The name arrives with two hazards worth naming. *Miscontrast* — 3.4× FCF looks extraordinary next to the register's typical 15–30× candidates, and the pull is to treat cheapness as evidence of quality; it is not, and the ten-year record is the answer. *Hope* — the FY2022–FY2025 recovery is genuine and invites extrapolating four good years over six bad ones; §5 forbids that by construction, since normalisation is multiyear averaging only, never selective windowing. Neither judgment here is born of a bias: the verdict rests on mechanical thresholds, and it would be identical at any price.

## D. Future-performance worksheet

**Breadth — customers.** PASS. TUI carried roughly 20 million holidaymakers in FY2025, each an individual consumer paying for their own trip. No customer-concentration disclosure exists because no customer approaches a tenth of revenue; the absence is meaningful here in the company's favour, the opposite of the usual reading.

**Breadth — suppliers.** PASS. The largest purchased input is hotel bed stock, bought across hundreds of independent hoteliers plus the group's own and joint-ventured properties; €415.2m of hotel services from related parties is 2.0% of cost of sales plus administrative expenses. Aircraft come from Boeing under a genuine duopoly, but that spend is capital expenditure, Airbus stands ready as an alternative, and the ready-alternatives exception in P15 covers it. Fuel, airport handling and ground transport are all multi-vendor.

**Forces.**

| Force | Call | Reason |
|-------|------|--------|
| Customer bargaining power | **STRONG** | Holidays are price-transparent and comparison-shopped; switching costs are zero from one booking to the next; consumers trade down or defer in any squeeze. |
| Supplier bargaining power | WEAK | TUI is one of the largest single buyers of European leisure bed stock, negotiating against a fragmented and capacity-long hotel sector. |
| Threat of substitutes | **STRONG** | All three of the book's forms are live: direct (self-assembled flight-plus-hotel through Booking, Expedia, Airbnb), doing-without (the holiday is the most deferrable large household purchase), and wholly-different-function (other discretionary spending). |
| Threat of new entrants | WEAK | Aircraft fleets, air operator certificates, insolvency-protection bonding and enormous seasonal working capital are real barriers to entry, and scale is a real barrier to success in integrated tour operation. |

**Tally: 2 strong → Future WEAK** under P16 without needing the moat or market calls. Both are recorded for completeness.

**Moat.** Source: **Brand**, supported by owned-capacity scale. TUI is among the most recognised holiday brands in Germany, the UK and the Nordics, and vertical integration lets it control quality end-to-end. Direction: **stable**, not strengthening — the brand sustains volume but has not sustained pricing power or margin, which is the test that matters. It is a real moat and an insufficient one.

**Market growth.** **MIXED.** European outbound leisure volumes have fully recovered and TUI's revenue is 24% above FY2019, but the underlying category grows at low single digits, is sharply cyclical, and faces a persistent structural shift from packaged to self-assembled travel.

- *Outside directors:* Supervisory Board fixed fees are €270.0k (chair, Dr Dieter Zetsche), €180.0k (deputy chair) and €90.0k (member), with no variable component per GCGC G.18.
