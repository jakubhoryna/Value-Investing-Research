## 1. Architecture & doctrine

**The three-layer model (Ch. 21, Introduction).** Top layer, permanent: *know what to do → do it → don't do anything else*. Middle layer: *do I understand it? → is it good? → is it cheap?* — three sequential gates, each requiring a yes before the next is asked; the first no ends the idea. Bottom layer: the specific parameters — six understanding elements, seven historic metrics, four forces, six moat sources, four SF indicators, four price metrics, 18 biases plus two forms of akrasia. Marshall states outright that the bottom layer is the part most likely to change as accounting standards and disclosure regimes evolve, and that keeping it current is one of the two practices that preserve the model (the other: think in percents, not currency amounts — discipline learned with little capital must work identically with much). The project's §3 tunable-parameter table is therefore book-sanctioned by design.

**Three disciplines (Introduction).** Finance reveals which companies have been operationally successful and are underpriced — accounting plus simple arithmetic. Strategy shows which successful pasts promise successful futures — qualitative positioning. Psychology supplies the restraint to reject judgments born of inborn biases. The model is interdisciplinary out of necessity, not decoration: a number-cruncher ignorant of biases, a strategist who can't read statements, and a psychologist blind to competitive advantage all fail. The middle gate ("know what to do") draws on finance and strategy; "do it" and "don't do anything else" draw entirely on psychology — buying at the precise moment everyone else sells demands an on-demand contrarian posture most people don't have, and refusing every non-value diversion is harder still.

**Known blind spot (Introduction).** Marshall concedes the model produces "particularly weird verdicts" on financial institutions and utilities — businesses it is structurally bad at assessing. The book's remedy is to accept these misses; it does not license a replacement gauge. The project's FI lens is a [PROJECT] departure, softer than the book, authorised by Jakub's J2/J4 (2026-10-06) and confined to licensed banks and insurers (§13 D23).

**Core definitions.** Value investing: acting on the observation of a clear difference between price and worth. Speculating: buying now in the hope of selling higher later with no consideration of why that might be possible. Risk: the chance of losing money — never volatility. Intrinsic value: the output of fundamental analysis, best handled as a *range* (Marshall's excess-cash agnosticism produces two bookend values; a good company priced well below the low end is a buy, one wildly above the high end a candidate sale — inability to pin a point value is no practical limitation). Margin of safety: the space allowed for being wrong; the discipline of waiting not for a little inexpensive but for *undeniably* inexpensive, which covers botched analysis and — counterintuitively — generates the uncommon upsides.

**Price and value are different (Ch. 3).** The Aqua Sphere goggles parable: a technically superior product's merits (comfort, watertightness, drag) are unchanged whether the price is bid to $35 by rumor or dumped to $9.99 in bargain bins — and companies are likewise judged on operating results, positioning, and shareholder orientation, none of which price movements alter. Price is an offer: take it or don't; it teaches nothing. A stock 50% off its high is evidence only that it is 50% off its high. The efficient-market concession: *over time* average price mirrors average worth; *at a point in time* it may not, and those points are when the value investor acts. Popular media routinely conflates the two (Apple's 2014 "worth more than $700bn" headlines priced Apple; whether they valued it was a separate question).

**Risk mechanics (Ch. 15, 21).** Buying inexpensively simultaneously raises return (a $4 value bought at $2 returns 100%; at $1, 300% — halving price triples return) and lowers risk, because risk is money at stake and paying less puts less at stake (the baseball leadoff: start closer to the goal). The volatility (σ) definition fails twice: past price movement doesn't govern future price movement (stocks have no inertia), and it produces absurdities — a share that ranged $1–3 is scored riskier than one that ranged $2–3 even for a buyer who paid the $1 low. Volatility persists because it is institutionally convenient: easier to manage than return. Value investing inverts the risk-return trade-off — outperformance happens *because of* lower risk, not despite it. Capital preservation is first because realized-loss arithmetic is punitive: −50% needs +100% to restore; unlike a missed shot in soccer, a missed shot in investing scores negative. Most actively managed equity funds trail basic indexes — the output of a majority that subordinates preservation to the trade-off myth.

**Why listed stocks (Ch. 2).** Every alternative asset class has a structural ceiling: bonds return at most principal plus interest (no issuer pays bondholders a bonus); currencies are eroded by inflation (the Balboa Bar: $1.25 in 1979 → $3.75 in 2016 ≈ 3% p.a., with after-tax savings interest unable to keep pace) and traded in the world's most liquid, mispricing-free market; commodities track inflation minus carrying costs; unlisted equity suffers selection bias — self-reported VC indices are the S&P 500 with the 495 laggards deleted (2014: 11% reported honestly becomes 101% counting only the top five). Stocks are claims on ceiling-free earnings. The value posture: treat 100 shares exactly as 100% of the business — fundamental analysis of the company, indifferent to charts and macro.

**Data doctrine (Ch. 2).** Annual reports and 10-K/20-F beat data services for two stated reasons: filings are accurate under penalty of law (an executive can go to jail; a publisher shielded by free-expression law merely loses subscribers), and services summarize narrative, which means they interpret — the investor should be the interpreter. Navigation rule: the prettier the page, the less useful; the meat starts at the consolidated financial statements. Quarterlies are unaudited and Q4 mops up the prior three quarters' estimates, so they serve only figures that must be *timely more than audited* — current share count, latest balance sheet for EV. The proxy (DEF 14A) carries comp, director fees, and conflicts. IR departments are a legitimate tool for questions with factual answers, and the quality and speed of the reply is itself information about the company.

**Measuring one's own performance (Ch. 4).** Three steps: per-year total return (numerator = capital appreciation + realized gains + dividends net of unreclaimable withholding + interest on investment-earmarked cash; denominator = holdings at year-start prices + cash deployed during the year — this construction neutralizes deposits and withdrawals); geometric mean across years (three-year minimum; the negative-year workaround: add 100% to each year, GEOMEAN, subtract 100%); comparison to one standard chosen once and kept (Marshall benchmarks the S&P 500 Total Return and holds that a skilled value investor can beat it by ~500 bps over time — the source of the 15% aspiration used in Ch. 15 and the twin-portfolio illustration in Ch. 17). Beware Washington Generals benchmarks — with 180,000+ indices on offer, one is always beatable. Uninvested earmarked cash: exclude it to compare against fully invested indexes, include it (with its interest) to measure sourcing ability; both defensible if consistent. Returns are pretax by necessity (rates vary too much to generalize) but tax matters and later nudges the whole strategy toward long holding.

## 2. Gate 0 — Understanding the business (Ch. 5)

To understand a business is to be able to describe it in a single unambiguous sentence in plain language — the understanding statement. Marshall's Wal-Mart template packs all six parameters into one line: the world's largest retailer (status) moving value-priced merchandise such as groceries (products) quickly through an efficient distribution network (operational form) via large stores mostly in North America (geography) to lower-income consumers (customers) in retailing (industry). The chance that its author doesn't understand the company is zero — that is the test.

**The six parameters, each with its reasoning:**

1. **Products.** First cut: goods vs services (Samsung vs Southwest — classify by the bulk of revenue, per the 10-K). Second: position on the commodity↔differentiated spectrum — a commodity is fully describable on paper (Horsehead's zinc: weight, shape, purity; the brand is irrelevant), a differentiated product is anything else (LVMH: look, feel, associations); the more the brand matters, the more differentiated. Hunt for **hidden products**: Vivint Solar's 10-K reveals a VP of capital markets and sponsored investment funds — its second product is solar investment *tax credits* sold to profitable entities. The labor pays off at the threat-scan stage: a hypothetical new tax-credit class (parking garages) would compete for Vivint's fund investors, a threat invisible if the product definition stopped at "residential solar services." Think plainly: Unilever's products are better called soap and food than fast-moving consumer goods.
2. **Customers.** Whoever *pays*, not whoever uses (Yahoo's users pay nothing; its advertisers are the customers). Consumers or organizations; as specific as the filings allow — Chico's "women" is correct, "middle-income women 35+" is better and obtainable from the 10-K plus a store visit. Wal-Mart's "lower-income consumers" comes straight from its own 10-K citing public-assistance payments as an operating driver — a line absent from midmarket Target's filing.
3. **Industry.** Usually obvious, sometimes layered: Avon is cosmetics *and* multilevel marketing — seeing the second layer reveals kitchen-tool MLMs as competitors.
4. **Form.** Legal and operational. Legal form is usually unremarkable (corporation, PLC, AG) but occasionally explanatory: Equity Residential's REIT status *requires* paying out 90% of taxable income — the form is the key to the dividend policy. Operational form: franchisor (Jack in the Box) vs franchisee (Carrols running Burger Kings), multilevel marketer, vertically integrated (SCA: Europe's largest forest owner also making diapers from its own fluff pulp — bizarre without the form lens, coherent with it). The Wal-Mart statement smuggles operational form in via "rushing" — the internal distribution network that drives profitability.
5. **Geography.** Customers, operations, and HQ — emphasize whichever is most enlightening. Exposes thorny issues: Philip Morris International is NYSE-listed and New York–headquartered but sells only outside the US, an arrangement that escapes home-country restrictions on a dangerous, addictive product (a moral dimension the investor must at least see); Medtronic's post-Covidien Irish domicile is a tax inversion — grasping the tax geography is part of grasping the company.
6. **Status.** Catchall for prominence, age, transformation, size. Sears: a onetime giant, by 2016 unprofitable, shrinking, closing stores — fading is a status. Size cuts both ways and the statement should surface it: big companies raise capital cheaply, launch with force, absorb losses for years, but can't change unnoticed, attract activists, and calcify into defensive postures; small ones exploit niches, keep secrets, and move fast.

**The four traps** — all are ways of mistaking a part for the whole: (1) **product familiarity** — firsthand customer knowledge is a good idea-source but is one parameter of six, not business understanding; (2) **marketing messages** — rallying cries aimed at customers, useful evidence, never ready-made statement modules; (3) **mission statements** — marketing pointed inward at employees; (4) **aspirations** — describe what the business *is*, not what it plans (Clas Ohlson announcing three Hamburg stores does not make its geography "Northern Europe"; plans change and expansions falter).

**Multi-unit rule.** If one unit dominates, define the six parameters for it alone; if units are more equally weighted, define all six per unit — and carry the split forward into the force calls (as the Kone case does with goods vs services).

**The hard stop and its reasoning.** When a business is sincerely hard to understand, either it genuinely exceeds one's competence or *someone doesn't want it understood* — obfuscation hoping unintelligibility will be mistaken for flair. Either way, no part of it. The famous inversion: the danger of investing in what one doesn't understand isn't so much losing money — it's *making* money, because the gain teaches false lessons about what "similar" means and tilts future capital toward look-alikes chosen blind. Circle of competence is real but dynamic: frozen at age twelve it would contain video games and chocolate; a stretch is an invitation to learn (proficiency accumulates even when the idea is ultimately dropped), while genuine incomprehension remains a wall.

## 3. The six key numbers (Ch. 6–10)

**Accounting is a convention, not physics (Ch. 6).** The casa blanca point: demanding that accounting square with some celestial bookkeeping truth is like citing Spanish for putting adjectives after nouns. US GAAP classing interest payments as operating cash flow has no deep logic — it is a standard, masterable like any other. Three statements matter. The income statement (accrual — the doorman making judgments) and cash flow statement (cash — the turnstile just counting, sorted into operations/investing/financing) are movies over a period; the balance sheet (assets = liabilities + equity) is a snapshot. Most listed companies build CFO by the indirect method (start at net income, adjust). Four measures of income descend the income statement: operating income → EBIT (± non-operating items) → pre-tax earnings (− interest) → net income (− tax). Goodwill in essence = acquisition price in excess of the target's equity (Marshall knowingly bypasses purchase-price allocation to identifiable intangibles). The LinkedIn 2015 statements are the worked fluency drill: revenue $2,990.9m; COGS labeled "cost of revenue (exclusive of D&A)"; operating expenses scattered across four lines summing to $2,723.0m; operating loss $(150.9)m; a three-line "other income (expense)" section to be sorted by hand into non-operating income vs interest expense; a negative tax expense; and equity of $4,468.6m — smaller than assets-minus-liabilities by a $26.8m *redeemable noncontrolling interest* (the China JV minority) that the prior year's filing explains and that the conservative investor excludes from book value. Abnormalities are normal; fluency is built on a thousand twists.

### 3.1 Capital employed (Ch. 7)

**Definition and philosophy.** CE is the required financial base — the resources that must stay in the business for it to maintain its level of operations. Like the border of Southern California, every investor's definition is subjective yet firmly held; considering it at all is what confers the advantage. Marshall's formula: total assets − excess cash − non-interest-bearing current liabilities (possibly − goodwill). The governing stance is **subtractive conservatism**: all assets are presumed needed unless proven otherwise; start from the huge number and chip away only what certainly isn't required. This is deliberately preferred to the additive invested-capital construction (equity + financial debt) because starting big and deducting selectively reduces the chance of *underestimating* the base — which would manufacture diamonds out of glass when returns are computed.

**The bicycle parable** (why CE is financing-blind and why leases are debt): a one-bike rental venture needs $1,000 for the bike — CE is $1,000 whether the money was borrowed or raised as equity. Raise $1,500 and the idle $500 is capital but not employed — excess. Now *lease* the bike instead: day one looks identical (empty bank account, bike ready to rent) but reported CE is zero — the obligation escaped the balance sheet. The lease-capitalization adjustment repairs exactly that misrepresentation.

**Excess cash is unknowable.** The set-percent-of-revenue school (e.g., cash above 5% of revenue is excess) is formula-worship — how much cash a firm needs depends on business model, customer payment speed, and vendor terms. Marshall's solution: compute **two versions** — all cash subtracted (CE_xc) and none (CE_wc) — and let the range carry through every downstream metric. (The centrifuge extension: idle *non-cash* assets are equally excess in principle but undetectable from a balance sheet; the point is noted, not solved.)

**NIBCL.** Accounts payable, deferred income, accrued expenses — debts about to be settled, which by definition needn't stay in the business. The *non-interest-bearing* qualifier exists to keep financial debt in: long-term borrowings slip into current liabilities as they mature without changing their nature as capital structure, so short-term debt and current maturities always remain in CE.

**Goodwill.** A judgment call keyed to recurrence: the more habitual the acquisitions, the less deducting makes sense — at Cisco (8, 13, and 7 acquisitions in successive years; goodwill +$2.3bn in 2014, nearly a quarter of total assets) goodwill is as ongoing as capex and clearly part of the employed base. Marshall's default: often leave it in.

**Boundaries.** Further balance-sheet fluffing (unfunded pensions etc.) is possible but there is a point where analysis cost exceeds decision value. CE is perishable — good for one balance-sheet date — and rests on historical costs that may diverge from replacement costs. Investing is a messy lesser art; it works because the only decisions are buy, sell, or nothing.

**Worked build — Gap FY2015 (10-K, balance sheet dated 2016-01-30).** Total assets $7,473m. Current liabilities sorted line by line: current maturities of debt — interest-bearing, *stays*; accounts payable $1,112m — the perfect NIBCL, subtract; "accrued expenses and other" — decomposed via its note into unredeemed gift cards (may never settle — stays), accrued compensation $230m (owed between pay periods — subtract), short-term deferred rent and tenant allowances (not clearly leaving — stays), and "other" (nebulous — the burden of proof rests with the item to warrant subtraction, so it stays); income taxes payable $23m — tax under discussion can behave like government credit, but *payable* sounds final, subtract. CE_wc = 7,473 − 1,112 − 230 − 23 = **$6,108m**; CE_xc = 6,108 − 1,370 cash = **$4,738m**. One may instead subtract all NIBCL without scrutiny — acceptable — but the closer look both avoids underestimating the base and teaches the business (the gift-card float *is* a Gap fact worth knowing).

### 3.2 Lease capitalization (Ch. 7–9)

**When.** Accounting distinguished operating leases (expense when due; off balance sheet) from capital/finance leases (on balance sheet, treated as purchase-with-debt; under US GAAP any one of four conditions triggers it — ownership transfer, bargain purchase option, term ≥ ¾ of useful life, PV of minimum payments ≥ 90% of fair value; IFRS: substance of risks and rewards). The investor's adjustment applies when accounting sees operating but the economics smell finance. The look-through logic: the lessor is both vendor and lender — it borrowed on the business's behalf to buy the asset. Post-ASC 842/IFRS 16 balance sheets capitalize natively, so the manual procedure now matters only for pre-adoption years inside the history window (project P18).

**Present value mechanics.** Liability (and equal asset) added = PV of scheduled lease payments. Inputs: the commitments table in the filing (five annual amounts plus a thereafter lump to be allocated by roughly extending the visible downtrend — even spreading is acceptable for small residuals) and a discount rate equal to the company's own most recent cost of debt, hunted via *long-term debt / interest rate / senior note* searches. Rate selection is judgment, not science — precision here is precision masquerading as accuracy, and a genuine bargain shines through a ±100 bp band; an opportunity that dies at one rate and lives at a neighboring one was never an opportunity.

**The six steps** (pretend the equipment was bought with borrowed money): (1) add the lease expense back on the income statement; (2) add the lease payments back on the cash flow statement; (3) add the PV liability and equal asset to the balance sheet; (4) expense imputed interest (rate × PV) on the income statement; (5) outflow imputed interest payments on the cash flow statement; (6) depreciate the lease asset. The standard simplifying assumption: new interest + new depreciation = old lease expense, so **net income is unchanged** and depreciation = lease expense − imputed interest. The master rule the project codifies as three-statement consistency: **adjustments shaping one number must shape the others** — CE, OI, and FCF move together or the ratios lie.

**Worked build — Gap FY2015.** Note 11 schedule: $1,135m / $1,098m / $946m / $821m / $682m for 2016–2020, then $2,118m for 2021–2032. Allocation by extending the downtrend: 500/400/300/200 for 2021–24, remaining $718m spread evenly at $89.75m across 2025–32. Rate: the 2011-vintage 5.95% note is stale; the 1% yen loan mismatches the dollar-funded property; the 1% October 2015 loan is too short — settle on **4%**. PV of the 17-year stream = **$5,693.2m** — which nearly *doubles* CE_wc (to $11,801.2m) and more than doubles CE_xc (to $10,431.2m). Nothing unusual for a retailer; the declining schedule does not mean Gap is shrinking, only that later-year leases weren't yet signed. (Non-US disclosure is often blurrier — H&M mashes years two-through-five together, forcing more guessing.)

### 3.3 Operating income (Ch. 8)

OI = revenue − COGS − operating expenses: the topmost income measure, chosen over net income because it strips what management can change by fiat — capital structure (Kroger's $443m interest on $9.7bn debt vs Whole Foods' zero) and tax regime (Kroger 32.9% vs Loblaw 26.6%, a policy gap, not a grocery gap) — leaving the fixed economics of the core operation. This enables the two comparisons the whole Past gate rests on: the same firm through time (relocations and recapitalizations drop out) and different firms at a point in time. The stipple-drawing metaphor: the core operation is the art; tax and capital structure are a replaceable frame, and someone will eventually reframe it — the investor's job is to appreciate the drawing and ready wall space. Marshall grounds the preference in two personal circumstances worth remembering when porting the model: American activism norms make tax/debt genuinely optimizable, and a decades-long holding period gives companies time to optimize — investors in cross-shareholding, custom-bound markets may justifiably weight net income more. Lease adjustment: OI_adj = published OI + lease expense − imputed depreciation. **Gap:** 1,524 + 1,313 − 1,085.27 = **$1,751.7m** (imputed interest 4% × 5,693.2 = $227.7m; depreciation 1,313 − 227.7 = $1,085.3m).

### 3.4 Free cash flow (Ch. 9)

FCF = CFO − **maintenance** capex, ideally. Only replacement outlays should be deducted, because FCF measures the cash-generating power of *present* operations; subtracting growth capex understates the business. Statements don't split capex, and the depreciation proxy is rejected for two argued reasons: (1) **inflation** — depreciation is a fraction of old cost, likely below replacement cost, risking overstated FCF; (2) **quality** — replacements differ in kind. The four-machines illustration: four $4,800 machines (4-year life, $1,200/yr each) produce year-4 depreciation of $4,800; replace one at end of year 4 with a $2,000 two-year machine of equal output and year-5 depreciation is $4,600 while actual maintenance capex was $2,000 — less than half. Direction of error is unknowable in advance, which is what disqualifies the proxy. The hunt order: a disclosed maintenance figure in the 10-K; failing that, the Q&A of earnings calls (prepared remarks are storytime; the Q&A unearths nuggets); failing both, subtract all capex knowing the picture is painted overly dark — assume the worst with the prospect of delight rather than the best with the likelihood of disappointment. A depreciation-to-total-capex comparison still calibrates *how* dark (Gap: dep $588m = 81% of $726m capex — understatement modest).

Lease adjustments mirror OI: + lease payments (assumed equal to lease expense, paid when due) − imputed interest payments. **IFRS trap:** filers that put interest paid in financing must have it moved into CFO before deriving levered FCF — and if leases are being capitalized for such a filer, both the existing and the new imputed interest belong in operations.

**Levered vs unlevered.** Levered (interest and tax in) is the book's working variant because it is the deliberate opposite lens to OI: cash-basis and post-interest-and-tax vs accrual and pre-both; together they bracket everything important about money coming out of a business. Unlevered = levered + all interest payments (existing and imputed) − incremental tax of (1 − effective rate) × interest added back (interest is deductible; erasing it raises taxable income). **Gap:** levered 1,594 − 726 + 1,313 − 227.7 = **$1,953.3m**; unlevered adds back 227.7 + 59 = 286.7 of interest, subtracts 62.5% × 286.7 = 179.2 of extra tax (effective rate 37.5%) = **$2,060.8m**.

**The normal cash cycle** — the reason FCF is structurally held to a lower benchmark than OI: most businesses pay before they are paid (space, wages, inputs precede customer cash), so revenue recognition leads cash inflow, especially while growing. Exceptions: shrinking firms (cash from old orders pours in as buying slows) and negative-cash-cycle models (prepaid magazine subscriptions), where unlevered FCF can exceed OI even in growth. Neither OI nor FCF is superior — considering both brings the knowable closer to the known.

### 3.5 Book values and shares (Ch. 10)

**BV** = equity **excluding noncontrolling interests** — the smaller number, the pie a shareholder can actually buy a slice of (LinkedIn's $26.8m China-JV NCI). Equity rises via retained earnings and stock issuance; falls via dividends and buybacks — so every BV-based metric is buyback-influenced, a fact to carry rather than fix. **TBV** = BV − all intangibles (goodwill, trade names, patents, customer relationships, non-competes — Gap's $277m across four note lines; indefinite-lived items escape amortization, finite-lived ones tick down). TBV is deliberately old-fashioned — the worth of the company dead — and its operational use is **flagging acquisition overpayers**: when BV ≫ TBV because goodwill is enormous, pull the acquired company's last standalone filings and ask, would I have paid that price? If not, stop — whatever the purported synergies. Overpriced acquisitions are the beginning of a story that ends poorly. (Gap: BV $2,545m, TBV $2,268m — no flag.)

**Shares.** Three counts: outstanding (basic) < fully diluted (+ everything exercisable/convertible) < authorized (+ everything issuable). Use **fully diluted, weighted average** for all per-share work: in a successful company anything that can become a share will become a share — employees exercise, bondholders convert, warrantholders exercise, all to sell at newly high prices. Authorized overshoots (issuing beyond conversions is a major discretionary act); the weighted average absorbs intra-year bopping. Known imperfection, lived with: dilution math ignores strike-price cash inflows. Finding it: search the filing for *diluted* — only chemical companies use the word otherwise (Gap: 413m).

## 4. Gate 1A — Past performance (Ch. 11)

**The seven metrics:** ROCE, FCFROCE, ΔOI/FDS, ΔFCF/FDS, ΔBV/FDS, ΔTBV/FDS, liabilities-to-equity. Only about a dozen inputs per year feed all seven: OI and possibly interest expense (income statement); CFO and capex (cash flow statement); cash, goodwill, intangibles, total assets, accounts payable, deferred income, accrued expenses, total liabilities, equity (balance sheet); plus fully diluted shares and maybe the effective tax rate. With a spreadsheet the whole table is manageable, and the breadth of history it reveals is the payoff.

**The denominator-timing problem and the four-way range.** OI is a movie; CE is a snapshot — which snapshot produced the year's income? Beginning of period (prior FY-end balance sheet), end of period, or their mean are all defensible; quarterly balance sheets are unaudited and excluded. Marshall computes beginning and end, each with and without cash — **four measures of ROCE and four of FCFROCE per period** — because ranges are honest reminders of how rough even careful calculations are. The operative test, demonstrated in the Gap case: hold the **worst of the four** against the benchmark; if the worst clears the bar, interest survives.

**Normalization.** Irregular years mislead in both directions, so judge normalized results — but normalize **only by multiyear averages**: average ROCE = Σ OI ÷ Σ CE across the window (per CE variant), growth by geometric mean (arithmetic means fail compounding). Never tweak individual years, for three stated reasons: (1) only future periods reveal what was truly one-off — a "one-time" litigation charge that recurs wasn't; (2) spotting genuine irregularities takes management-level knowledge, and filings are just detailed enough to fool outsiders into thinking they have it; (3) every year has irregularities, so justifiable tweaking never ends. Historic performance is exactly that — historic; don't sanitize it and pretend the sanitized version repeats. **L/E is the exception: never normalized** — only the latest reading matters, since a lower ratio last year makes today's obligations no lighter.

**Benchmarks and their derivations:**

| Metric | Bar | Derivation |
|---|---|---|
| ROCE (worst of four averages) | ≥ 15% | The S&P 500 total return since 1967 has a geometric mean of 10.2% — obtainable passively via an index fund, so it is the opportunity cost. The delisting thought experiment: buy the whole company, delist it, and over the years your return converges on income relative to capital employed (with depreciation eventually approximating maintenance capex). Below 10.2%, the index fund wins. ROCE's numerator excludes interest and tax, so the bar is grossed up: 15% ≈ the S&P's return if its constituents paid neither. Why price paid doesn't break the comparison: inflation makes the historic purchase price fade — $7m paid for $1m of OI feels like a full multiple today and looks cute three decades later when OI is $2.5m on inflation alone (~3% p.a.). |
| FCFROCE (worst of four) | ≥ 8% | Same market-return logic, haircut for the normal cash cycle — FCF structurally lags OI in a growing business, so it is held to a lower standard. |
| ΔOI/FDS, ΔFCF/FDS (geo-mean) | ≥ ~3% nominal | US inflation has averaged ~3% for decades; financial statements are nominal, so sub-3% nominal growth is a business shrinking in real terms. Explicitly a floor: 4% is no growth story, higher benchmarks are appropriate, and opinions vary as to how much higher. |
| L/E (latest year) | ≤ 2, hard ceiling 7 | See below. |

**Leverage.** L/E = **total liabilities** ÷ BV — the numerator maniacally includes everything owed, interest-bearing or not, because companies can crumble under the weight of obligations of any kind; the goal is the company in the dimmest possible light. Interest coverage (EBIT ÷ interest) and financial-debt-to-equity are acceptable alternatives — what matters is considering indebtedness in some serious way. The reasoning: liabilities amplify. Lenders and vendors have capped upside (interest, principal, invoices), so success gushes to the ceiling-free shareholders; failure meets a liability burden that lets up not one iota, and insolvency is the technical possibility that tilts value investors toward manageable obligations. Above 2 is palatable only in two cases, sometimes combined: a very creditworthy company borrowing cheap money for a high-return use, and equity shrunk by distributions — the archetype being cheap debt buying back clearly underpriced stock, where debt soars, book condenses, and a grotesque ratio can mark an attractive situation. Ceiling 7 even then. Capitalized-lease PV belongs in the numerator when the analysis has capitalized leases (Gap: 194% as reported, 417% lease-fluffed).

**ΔBV and ΔTBV** have limited standalone use for two reasons: dividends come straight out of book value (and a dollar of dividend ≠ a dollar of retained book growth, not least because one is usually taxed), and buybacks can force the metrics negative while OI/share grows. They are still computed — recognizing a metric as unhelpful in a particular case is itself a finding, and the *reason* it's unhelpful (payout posture, repurchase scale) is exactly what Gate 1C needs.

**The discipline.** Avoid making excuses for unremarkable companies — the model is designed to flag them; if a business looks bad, consider it no further. And remember the tense: metrics say something *grew* at 10%, not that it *is growing* at 10%.

**Worked record — Gap, five years 2011–2015 (all figures lease-capitalized).** Five-year OI sum $10,345.4m; four-year (for beginning-CE variants, since beginning-of-2011 CE would need a 2010 balance sheet not pulled) $8,643.3m. CE sums: end-wc $55,993.1m, end-xc $48,253.1m, beg-wc $44,191.9m, beg-xc $37,821.9m — numerator years always matching denominator years. Four ROCE averages: **18 / 21 / 20 / 23%** — worst 18%, above the 15% bar, still interested. FCF sums $10,521.6m / $8,783.7m → FCFROCE **19 / 21 / 20 / 23%** — worst 19% vs an 8% bar, wildly clear. Growth (geo-means of four YoY observations): ΔOI/FDS **7%** but −19% in 2015; ΔFCF/FDS **10%**, −17% in 2015; ΔBV/FDS **4%** (buybacks and dividends both confirmed tamping it — the "book value thieves"), −9% in 2015; ΔTBV/FDS **3%**, −11% in 2015. L/E: liabilities $4,928m ÷ equity $2,545m = **194%** — not alarming in a 1%-borrowing-rate context with a buyback-shrunk denominator; 417% with the lease PV added. The picture the numbers paint: a highly profitable business that stopped growing — so its attractiveness hinges on whether growth returns, which is precisely the question the next gate exists to answer. The numbers not only tell a story; they say where to look next. (Marshall's coda: readers may dispute his discount rates and choices — hundreds of tiny judgments feed one company's quantitative analysis; what matters is laying the choices plain so others can make their own.)

## 5. Gate 1B — Future performance (Ch. 12)

Accounting for future periods doesn't exist, so the assessment is strategic, via four qualitative tools. **Quantitative forecasting is rejected** for two reasons: predicting prices, costs, and shares is guesswork cloaked in mathematics; and dummied-up statements look official enough to make one mistake precision for accuracy. Owning good operating histories in solid strategic positions is the reliable route to futures that play out well. The four tools overlap by design (supplier breadth ≈ supplier power; moats draw on substitutes) — redundancy lowers the odds of missing something.

### 5.1 Breadth analysis

Two questions; the business isn't good unless both answers are yes. **Customer base broad and unlikely to consolidate:** no single customer over a tenth of revenue. The logic by illustration: two customers at half of sales each means losing one devastates revenue regardless of cause or fault; a thousand at a thousandth each means a loss barely registers. The test is documentary — annual reports often state that no customer exceeded 10% of revenue, and the absence of the sentence suggests the absence of the condition. **Supplier base broad and unlikely to consolidate:** no single supplier over a tenth of COGS or operating expenses. Reports dance around this, so reason from what the business must buy: must inventory be a specific brand? do key components come from a one-family economy? is labor scarce and unionized or plentiful and trainable? One exception: deliberate concentration for bulk discounts and service concessions still passes **if several potential vendors stand ready to serve** — alternatives keep the favorite on best behavior. Two traps: nominal counts ("sources from over 100 suppliers" is compatible with half of purchases from one of them), and surface-level supplier identification — **look through to the effective supplier**. Delta Financial (2007): a subprime mortgage lender, understood, historically fine; its product was money and the buyers of its securitized loans looked like a broad supplier base — until the residential-mortgage-backed-securities *market itself* was recognized as the single supplier of its funding. Pass. Months later the market seized and Delta went bankrupt. No rate forecasts, no default modeling — a precarious circumstance spotted with a blunt, reliable tool.

### 5.2 Forces analysis

Marshall's company-level rendition of Porter's 1979 industry model (Porter's profitability measure, ROIC, is nearly Marshall's ROCE). Four forces, rivalry dropped — its substance lives inside direct substitutes. Each force is called **STRONG or WEAK, binary: medium isn't useful, because they're all kind of medium** — just decide which side of the line each sits on, one line of reasoning per call.

1. **Bargaining power of customers** — would clients get lower prices or faster delivery if they demanded them? Weakened by: many customers (a breadth pass nearly settles it), improbable backward integration (the customer would need a competence far from its core), high switching costs. Contrast pair: West Marine (2011) — boaters showroomed in branches and bought online, forcing discounts; no integration threat but zero switching costs and endless alternatives → strong, and the stock went nowhere for five years. International Flavors & Fragrances — once a proprietary compound is specified into a branded food or soap formula, the manufacturer's own customers expect that sensation → pricing power over time, customers weak.
2. **Bargaining power of suppliers** — mirror image: many suppliers, improbable forward integration, low switching costs for the company. Kraft Heinz: commodity inputs (milk, sugar, tomatoes) from a broad global base; any vendor failing on schedule or cost is replaced → weak, and the positive OI partly traces to it.
3. **Threat of substitutes** — three forms: direct (competitors' similar products), doing-without (an economics concept — rationally accepting theft losses instead of buying the security system), and wholly-different-function (a fence, a guard, heat sensors versus Axis's surveillance cameras). Kept low by substitutes offering lower value or by high adoption switching costs.
4. **Threat of new entrants** — start-ups *and* new divisions of incumbents; equals **barriers to entry plus barriers to success** — an entrant that can open shop but can't meaningfully penetrate is no threat. Any one of three attributes dulls it: scale economies, customer switching costs, hard-to-get permits.

**Decision rule:** all four weak → further consideration warranted; **two or more strong → stop**; exactly one strong → the judgment zone that experience makes tractable. When in doubt, quit — there are other companies. GameStop (early 2015): Sony, Microsoft, and Nintendo each supplied over 10% of new inventory purchases and every release *had* to be stocked → supplier power strong; digital distribution was visibly displacing physical media → substitutes strong; two strong forces, considered no further (stock subsequently fell, though two years is thin validation and Marshall says so).

### 5.3 Moat identification

A moat is a barrier that *lasts*. Moats are rare — most businesses have none, and finding strong historic metrics behind one is an infrequent event. A real moat has a **single identified source** among six; naming it is the test that separates a moat claim from wishful thinking:

| Source | Mechanism | Exemplar | Outsider verification |
|---|---|---|---|
| Government | Exclusive rights granted by the state | Weyerhaeuser: 15–25-year renewable provincial licences to manage 14m acres of Canadian forest — a competitor waits years just to apply | Read the licence terms |
| Network | Product benefit derived from other users; accumulation locks users in | Facebook: leaving costs the accumulated circle of contacts; page views sold to advertisers | Rival launch is technically easy, prying users away isn't |
| Cost | Structurally lower production cost from proprietary tech, method, or quirk of history | POSCO: deepwater shoreline land *gifted* by the Korean state — iron ore and coal arrive without inland transport; U.S. Steel bought its land and can never match it | Industry cost-per-ton rankings |
| Brand | Customers rarely consider substitutes | Nestlé's Maggi: over half the Indian instant-noodle market; the brand name *is* the category word | **Unaided recall**: ask target-market people "what's your favorite instant noodle?" without saying the name — market share alone could be promotional pricing |
| Switching costs | Migration is expensive enough to retain | Oracle: years of data, deep integration; leaving means new hardware, retraining, downtime, reintegration | Estimate the migration bill |
| Ingrainedness | Brand for the *channel* — so integral to a value chain the industry is hard to picture without it | Geberit: trains and certifies plumbers, distributes free planning software that specifies Geberit parts automatically | **Open-ended questions to channel participants** — see if the name comes up unprompted; invisible unless you're a European plumber |

State whether the moat is strengthening, stable, or eroding. In the v2 instructions, a dominant moat is *evidence for weak force calls* — the book's own reducers — not a separate override.

### 5.4 Market growth assessment

A basic directional sense of demand suffices — exact rates are neither required nor useful; an elementary belief that population grows can be enough. The tool doubles as a checkpoint: by this late stage an idea has developed a glow, and growth assessment is where enthusiasm gets a ready stage — the antidote is realism about upward-sloping curves. A flat market behind a strong moat can beat a growing contested one.

### 5.5 Worked walk-through — Kone (goods × services, segment-level calls)

Finnish elevator/escalator maker-and-servicer, top five globally, over a third of sales from China; conservative-assumption ROCE averaging over 20% and ΔFCF/FDS over 10% — quality worth stress-testing. **Breadth:** the annual report's customer sentence ("large number… no individual customer representing a material share") is imperfect — *material* isn't defined as 10% — but adequate; building owners won't consolidate globally. Supplier side needs reasoning: goods inputs are commodity materials (rubber, glass, steel) plus component subcontractors — many of both; service input is labor — trained in months-to-a-year, not doctor-length, unionized in places but expandable — just broad enough. Clean bill. **Forces, goods:** customer power weak (many; developers won't build elevators — competence too far from real estate; capital-equipment bidding is competitive but on balance weak); supplier power weak (fragmented, no forward-integration picture, and the report itself touts readiness to shift critical-component manufacturing between lines and suppliers — low switching costs); substitutes **strong** — direct rivals Otis, Schindler, ThyssenKrupp are real (doing-without fails: tall buildings plus disability law mandate elevators; wholly-different-function fails: helicopters and scaffolding aren't practical); new entrants weak — elevator failure modes (injury, panic, death) bias buyers toward safety reputations, a barrier to *success* (Marshall's own touché: what if GE or Siemens entered? — his weak call is opinion, and subjectivity is inescapable; that's investing). **Forces, services:** customer power weak (year-one maintenance bundled with equipment; commercial contracts follow; renewal rates high — Kone knows its own products and holds proprietary-parts access); supplier power weak (technicians can defect to start repair shops but not en masse; replacing one costs little beyond sunk training); substitutes weak (independents exist but bundling and renewals confine the direct threat to contract seams; doing-without is largely *illegal* — governments mandate inspections, and deferred maintenance can suspend building use; no different-function substitute); new entrants **strong locally** — a skilled technician starts a service outfit with little capital, low barriers to entry *and* success in a community — proven by Kone's own behavior: 23 acquisitions in 2015 and 17 in 2014, mostly regional maintenance firms. **Tally: exactly one strong force per segment**, each contained (bidding intensity in goods; market-by-market service entry) — the judgment zone, resolved favorably. **Moat:** switching costs, single source — proprietary parts and superior technical knowledge on both sides; fully exiting Kone means ripping out the installed unit. (Government looks like a source but isn't: agencies mandate maintenance without naming the provider — unlike the provinces naming Weyerhaeuser.) **Market growth:** population, plus urbanization (expensive city land → taller buildings) and aging (stairs get harder → single-floor living). **Method lessons the case teaches:** IR e-mail with factual questions (share of new equipment sold with service contracts, contract length, unionization) — Kone's exemplary speed and thoroughness properly colored the view, since how IR answers is part of the answer; and cold-calling a person in the target market (the maintenance chief of a multi-building owner) with candor about knowing nothing — which yielded a torrent no brokerage report could match. Listening is among the investor's most valuable tools; only time separates the willing listener from insight.

## 6. Gate 1C — Shareholder-friendliness (Ch. 13)

A shareholder-friendly company works primarily for the benefit of its outside owners — it sends its free cash flow to investors in one form or another. Assessment is qualitative, four indicators, **no fixed thresholds**; not every indicator needs equal depth in every case — look at what's glaring — but the verdict is **defined by the worst: one dicey indicator is enough**, never a four-way average, because the fair does nothing to mitigate the danger of the foul. The operative question: do I want to be in business with these people? And the burden of proof sits with the company — it is not for the investor to find a reason to call a factor bad; it is for the company to prove the factor is good (the sentence that decides the Swatch case).

**1. Compensation & ownership.** The ideal paradigm: reasonable executive salaries, normal part-time director fees, and insiders whose holdings — bought with their own cash at market prices — constitute the bulk of their net worths. Reality departs: eight-figure packages are routine, director fees push mid six figures, and option plans let insiders capture upside with no downside — any claim that they align incentives is laughable. Mechanics: search the proxy for the **summary compensation table** (a third of a 50-page US proxy may be comp narrative; the table cuts through) and take the far-right total for the highest-paid employee — the fattest number, composition irrelevant, because packages are dressed up like cheese at the end of a mouse maze. Strikes: **> $30,000,000, or > 5% of the lower of levered FCF and net income** — revenue is irrelevant; an executive's house size needn't scale with sales. Highest-paid *outside* director (not a full-time employee — often the non-executive chair or lead independent): norm ≈ **$250,000, stop above twice that**. Both figures are explicitly American and would be absurd in most other countries (Norwegian large-cap directors commonly earn under $100k) — scale to local context. Why care when comp is an EPS rounding error ($5m vs $10m CEO pay is undetectable against $1bn of income)? Because **comp is a window into top-level decision-making**: boards that tolerate outsized pay are less willing to jeopardize their remunerative posts by opposing a colleague's pet deal — they consent to overpriced acquisitions, and paying too much for management may be bearable, but paying too much for acquisitions can kill companies. The stated trade-off: favoring properly paid teams misses some opportunities and avoids more catastrophes. Ownership signals, in order: significant outright holdings by executives/directors are the positive (search *beneficial*; **ignore investment funds** on the chart — they don't run the company); open-market insider buying is promising; option exercises are neutral (non-open-market acquisitions generally are exercises); sales are never positive but rarely conclusive — tuition, mortgages, and founder selling plans are legitimate — unless several insiders dump most of their shares at once.

**2. Related-party transactions.** A related party is one whose dealings with the company could pose a conflict of interest. Not automatically bad — fine at arm's length, the metaphorical distance restricting purchases to what's needed at market rates. The rule: number, size, and **opacity** of dealings are inversely correlated with shareholder-friendliness — one or two small ones, extensively detailed, rarely hurt. Search the proxy for *related / relationships*; the taxonomy is leasing, consulting, hiring. Leasing worked example — H&M: the chairman's entities lease stores to the company for SEK 436m (2015); is it market rate? Store sizes aren't given (addresses are — good disclosure), so apply the **blunt fairness test**: insider share of the relevant total expense line — SEK 436m of SEK 20,554m total rent = 2%; supporting the chairman's real estate is clearly not a primary function of H&M, and with the address disclosure the setup troubles less. Consulting — Costco paid a director $300,000 to consult with few details: a negative by itself, but sometimes the right move is to file a fact away and judge it in the context of less ambiguous indicators. Hiring/dynasties — H&M's CEO is the founder's grandson; Fox and Cenveo seat sons of chairmen: Sweden reads dynasties as benevolent monarchies, America as meritocracy violations; Marshall's position — questionable, not disqualifying, because unhealthy nepotism surfaces elsewhere, in ROCE or in comp; stay alert for sour results without assuming familiar last names mean dopes. Boilerplate fairness assurances ("ordinary course… market rates") are thin, and that's tolerable: the goal isn't knowing everything, it's avoiding blatant plunderage — undeserved bonuses masquerading as something else. Subsidiary transactions don't count even where local definitions sweep them in.

**3. Share repurchases.** Positive **only at a discount to worth**. Bargain buybacks are delightful in every respect: the price-below-worth investment the investor would make personally, and tax-efficient — shrinking the claimant count urges the price up and pays shareholders in an untaxed, time-shiftable way (an investor chooses the low-income year or low-tax jurisdiction to realize; dividends arrive on the issuer's schedule). At any other price they are as destructive as overpriced acquisitions — they effectively *are* overpriced acquisitions. The perverse incentives: EPS-triggered bonuses can be juiced by shrinking the denominator with free cash flow even when income is flat, and option plans compound it — buybacks push the price up into the exercise window. Accounting is complicit twice: a repurchase touches the balance sheet (cash down, equity down) and the financing section, but **never the income statement — no mechanism exists to flag the overpayment when it happens**; and buybacks mechanically turbocharge ROCE (denominator drops, numerator doesn't), flattering the exact metric the quality gate leans on. The option-sterilization justification — repurchasing to offset plan issuance so diluted shares stay flat — Marshall nominates as one of the worst rationales in the history of finance: using company cash to move shares from outsiders to insiders is theft. Search term: *repurchase*; treasury-share notes carry the counts and the cash flow statement the cost.

**4. Dividends.** Drawbacks first: taxable, on the issuer's timing not the shareholder's; and a payout can signal exhausted reinvestment — a firm with high-return internal uses would logically plow earnings back, so persistent payers may be admitting they can't (the standing explanation for Silicon Valley's non-payers). Dividends are most shareholder-friendly from **fully priced companies in mature industries with long histories of strong free cash flow** — where retention has no superior use and buybacks would be overpriced. They are a capital-allocation choice among alternatives (repurchases, growth capex, acquisitions), which is why boiling them to a yield statistic oversimplifies; weigh them against local custom and law — Europe often pre-announces payout ratios, Germany permits dividends only from profitable companies, some US firms borrow to maintain theirs.

**Worked contrast — Swatch vs Fossil (same industry, opposite profiles; both had earlier-gate issues — smartphone-era market growth is iffy — and are studied for the contrast, not as survivors).** *Swatch:* CEO CHF 6.88m — above the Swiss average, below the American; chairwoman CHF 4.42m but that's a full-time executive post; highest-paid *outside* director CHF 175k — fine. Ownership: dual share classes (bearer = 5× the economic interest of registered); insiders hold the equivalent of 11.34m bearer shares against a weighted diluted bearer-equivalent count of 54.32m — **21% of the company**, a lot. RPT: the Hayek Group — owned by the heirs of the founder, which presumably includes the chairwoman and CEO — invoiced **CHF 10.2m** primarily for "support for Group Management": construction project management, investment-project assessment, cost control, IT consulting, audit, feasibility, process optimization, and *executive functions*. Aren't those what management is already paid to do? No malfeasance is alleged — and none needs to be: the company failed to prove the factor good. Buybacks trivial (< 1% of the company across three years; CHF 28m in 2015). Dividends CHF 407m — the main return channel. Verdict: **not shareholder-friendly** — the RPT is the glaring strike, executive comp above local norms is the aggravator the RPT's tone makes unignorable, and the big insider ownership is the noted positive that cannot mitigate (worst-defines). *Fossil:* highest-paid officer an EVP at $4.27m — unexceptional by US standards; the CEO's total comp for each of the last three years was **zero**, and he owns 12.5% of the company outright (directors and officers 14% together); lead independent director $235k — fine. RPT: a director's son employed in the Asia-Pacific division at $260k salary — a good wage, nothing screaming impropriety. No dividends, ever. Buybacks massive: roughly **a fifth of the company over three years for over $1bn, average ≈ $103/share** — combined with zero dividends, a management convinced repurchases are the far-and-away best use of FCF. Verdict: **contingent** — friendly only if $103 turns out to be well under per-share worth, which is exactly the question the price gate answers next. The case teaches the project's SF workflow: proportionate depth (chase what jumps out), quantified comp tests with local calibration, the blunt RPT expense-share test, buyback prices held against worth, and one-strike finality.

## 7. Gate 2 — Inexpensiveness (Ch. 14)

Nothing is worth infinity: even the best company becomes a bad investment at some price. Four price metrics, explicitly **not of equal rank**.

**MCap/FCF ≤ 8 — primary.** Market cap (shares × price; from a quote or built from the latest 10-Q share count) over levered FCF. The pairing is deliberately consistent: market cap prices the *equity only*, and levered FCF — post-interest — is the cash that *goes to* the equity holders. Two caveats the book supplies: market cap actually understates the takeover price, because the last trade reflects only the most willing sellers — Microsoft had to pay $196 for LinkedIn when the screen said $135, coaxing holdouts with higher reservation prices; and MCap-based multiples in general are foolable by capital structure (a leveraged company looks cheap because the numerator ignores the debt one would inherit) — which is why MCap/FCF is dangerous alone and paired with EV/OI.

**EV/OI ≤ 7 — primary, the "particularly sage" metric.** EV is the theoretical takeover price — pay it and no financial claimant remains: no common or preferred holders, no minority partners, no lenders. (Marshall notes the naming irony: it is derived from market prices, so *enterprise price* would fit value-investing vocabulary better.) EV = market cap + preferred equity + noncontrolling interest + all interest-bearing debt − cash. Why NCI is added: under consolidation accounting (ownership > 50%; cost method < 20%, equity method 20–50% with proportionate earnings-in-affiliate), the income statement mashes in 100% of the subsidiary's revenue and expenses and only corrects *below* operating income with the earnings-attributable-to-NCI line — the top of the statement dreams full ownership and the bottom wakes it up — so OI reflects a fiction of 100% ownership and the EV numerator must be fluffed as if there were no minority. Why compute EV from scratch rather than take website figures: sites auto-subtract *all* cash — a decision the analyst never made, which flatters the multiple and risks overpaying — and balance sheets can hide multiple cash-like lines (current financial assets, investment securities) whose true liquidity the footnotes must certify; when in doubt, assume not cash-equivalent. Excess cash being unknowable, compute **EV both ways** (all cash excess / none) and **don't buy before both versions put the company squarely in the bargain bin** — a good opportunity may slip away; a disaster won't sneak in. EV/OI is sage because, like CE, it is blind to financing: it prices everything and weighs it against pre-interest, pre-tax accrual earnings. The pairing rationale, verbatim in spirit: EV/OI is accrual-basis and pre-interest-and-tax; MCap/FCF is cash-basis and post-both — using both makes it harder to get bamboozled.

**Multiples are shorthand for a DCF.** $1m of OI annually for 100 years discounted at 10% ≈ $10m — i.e., EV/OI of 10 encodes "flat earnings, 10% discount rate"; the same 10 also encodes "4% growth, 14% discount rate." A low multiple therefore means a high implied discount rate on the income stream, and a low multiple signals a buying opportunity precisely when that implied rate is *unjustifiably* high. Exact decompositions are unknowable and unnecessary; the mapping is what matters.

**MCap/BV ≤ 3 and MCap/TBV ≤ 3 — qualifiers, the dead-company metrics.** They multiply what the company *has*, not what it produces; their honest reference point is liquidation, and the investor is buying a claim on future income streams, not a stake in a yard sale. Marshall is explicit that 3-and-3 are qualifiers — not what he looks for; the flow metrics are. Diagnostics, not gates: **BV reasonable, TBV stratospheric** → goodwill is enormous → audit the acquisition history (the Ch. 10 would-I-have-paid stop; a write-down is the market's eventual verdict on overpayment, and TBV prices the write-down in advance). **Both high and similar** → hunt for understated tangible assets — the EV/OI-9-with-both-book-ratios-at-6 pattern invites checking for land carried at a decades-old cost whose sale could flood the company with excess cash and collapse every multiple. Both are distorted *identically* by buybacks and dividends, so the informative quantity is their gap, not their level. Standing warning from the Preface: Marshall's self-declared worst error was selling Nike in 2010 because P/B crossed 3 — while knowing the swoosh sat on the books at exactly zero; earnings and price soared afterward. Book-value metrics must never drive decisions on asset-light franchises.

**Benchmark flexibility.** The model's metrics sort into three categories — return (ROCE, FCFROCE), growth (the four Δ/FDS), price (the four multiples) — and strength in two can compensate for averageness in the third: a higher EV/OI is acceptable when ROCE and growth are high; modest growth is palatable when ROCE is high and EV/OI low. No formula, for two argued reasons: all the metrics are historical (the strategic tools that address the future are qualitative and yield nothing pluggable), and category weights depend on holding period — a decades-long holder lives on return metrics, a flipper only on price. Hence: slight, constructive benchmark bending. (The project quantifies this as P12: one *primary* to ≤ 1.25× threshold, only when Past = STRONG.)

**Backvaluing.** For companies in *definitively* cyclical industries whose share prices plunge on down-cycle results: weigh today's MCap and EV against the prior cycle's FCF and OI. Insisting that trough results are permanent is conservatism of an unhelpful sort — but the tool assumes history repeats, the business is unchanged, and the cycle isn't secular decline. A tourniquet: unsafe outside a narrow set of circumstances; if there's any doubt about the cyclicality, don't.

**Why valuation comes last.** Two reasons. Objectivity: ignorance of price keeps the earlier gates honest — knowing a stock looks cheap tempts one to soften the new-entrant call; knowing it looks dear tempts one to invent a consolidating supplier base. Durability: understanding and quality findings have shelf life and can be called into service whenever a price takes a sharp turn — and sharp turns come from nowhere. Kone again: Toshiba bought 4.6% in 2001; its April 2015 accounting scandal forced non-core disposals; on July 22 it dumped the Kone stake and the stock fell 8% in a week — Japanese accounting trouble repricing Finnish elevators. Investors preloaded with the analysis grasped the situation faster than those starting cold. This is the register's entire theory of operation.

**Waiting.** When a good, understood company isn't cheap: wait, for years if necessary. Two supports. Action is not progress — acting feels productive and gives a jolt, but acting for its own sake isn't helpful. And cash has option value: holding it means being able to buy at the fleeting moments good companies get cheap; not holding it then leaves three ugly choices — miss the moment, sell another holding and its upside, or buy on margin. The option value of cash is never explicitly presented — no monthly statement congratulates flexibility — which is exactly why the discipline is unintuitive and must be deliberate. Once developed, it converts valuation into a standing order: *what would the stock price have to sink to such that MCap/FCF ≤ 8 and EV/OI ≤ 7? That becomes the limit price. We wait for it.* This sentence is the direct ancestor of the register's trigger band — computed on the two flow metrics alone.

### 7.1 Worked walk-through — Flowserve (the full Gate-2 mechanics)

Pumps, valves, and seals for refineries and industrial plants; assume understood and good; price at writing **$45.50**. Market cap: weighted average shares 129.781m (from the 10-Q's EPS note, found by searching *common stock*) × $45.50 = **$5,905.0m**.

**MCap/FCF.** CFO 2015 $417.1m; capex $181.9m with no maintenance split disclosed. The earnings call helps: the CFO states 2016 capex will decline from the *elevated* 2015 level, which funded Asia-Pacific capacity and an aftermarket licence — so some of 2015 was definitely growth. Sizing it: prior-year capex ran ≈ $135m and 2015 depreciation was $99.5m, so build two estimates — all-capex (conservative) and capex-minus-$45m (knocking it back to the prior-year level). Leases: schedule $45.5m / $37.6m / $28.4m / $22.1m / $18.7m for 2016–20, thereafter $63.8m with no end year — continue the visible 15–20% annual decline (15/12/10/8/6/5/4, remainder $3.8m in 2028). Rate: the 2015 €500m note at ~1.25% is current, and unlike Gap's yen loan the currency matches leased European operations — but 1.25% feels low; judgment settles on **2%**. PV of the 13-year stream = **$198.8m**; imputed interest 2% × PV = **$3.98m**. FCF_low = 417.1 − 181.9 + 53.1 (rental expense added back) − 4.0 = **$284.4m**; FCF_high = **$329.4m**. → **MCap/FCF ≈ 18–21.**

**EV/OI.** EV components from the 10-Q balance sheet: NCI $18.3m; debt $62.6m short + $1,573.5m long; cash $310.3m. EV_low (all cash excess) = **$7,249.1m**; EV_high (none) = **$7,559.4m**. OI 2015 published $525.6m; lease-adjusted: + 53.1 − depreciation 49.1 (= 53.1 − 3.98) = **$529.5m**. → **EV/OI ≈ 14 on both EV variants** (cash is small, so they converge).

**Qualifiers.** BV $1,722.7m → **MCap/BV = 3** — at the line, and bloated: Flowserve repurchased ~12% of itself over three years, which inflates both book ratios identically. TBV = 1,722.7 − goodwill 1,240.2 − other intangibles 228.3 = **$254.2m** → **MCap/TBV = 23**, wildly above 3. The two book ratios diverge vastly → goodwill is enormous → even if the price plummets, don't buy before auditing whether the acquisitions were overpaid. The qualifier breach mandates an investigation; it does not enter the band.

**Verdict and limit price.** Nothing screams inexpensive: 18 vs 8, 14 vs 7. Assume for the exercise the acquisition audit passes; fiddle the share price in a spreadsheet until both primaries come into line: Flowserve is inexpensive at **$17** — the limit price, computed on the two flow metrics alone, with MCAP/TBV = 23 treated as an investigation trigger and never a band input.

**Backvaluing.** Flow control for oil and gas is definitively cyclical (Palo Alto gasoline: $4/gal spring 2014 → $2.50 end-2015), and 2015 results sit in the trough. Rerun on 2014: CFO $571.0m; capex $132.6m against depreciation $93.3m → estimate maintenance at $120m; lease payments $56.2m; 2014 schedule ($49.6/36.8/27.8/22.1/17.2m, thereafter $63.8m spread down the trend) discounted at **4%** (the timely reference: a $300m note issued November 2013 at 4%) → PV $185.5m, interest $7.4m. FCF_2014 = 571.0 − 120 + 56.2 − 7.4 = **$499.7m** → backvalued MCap/FCF = **12**. OI_2014 = 789.8 + 56.2 − 48.8 = **$797.3m** → backvalued EV/OI = **9–10** across the EV variants. Less expensive against the good year — still not inexpensive. The backvalued limit price is **$32**, valid only if three conditions hold: the acquisition audit clears, the business hasn't changed between the cycle's years, and the industry is definitively cyclical rather than secularly declining. The astute investor — knowing price and worth differ, that action isn't progress, and that cash has option value — waits for a price no higher than that.

## 8. Psychology — misjudgment and misaction (Ch. 16)

**Lineage and stance.** Marshall's arc: trained in rational-actor economics, jolted by Black Monday 1987, converted as Kahneman–Tversky's prospect theory and behavioral economics rose and the efficient market *theory* was demoted to hypothesis. Value investors were never shocked — Graham and Dodd wrote of human-nature factors from the start, and Graham personified moodiness as Mr. Market. Marshall's declaration: forced to keep either psychological awareness or quantitative skill, he'd keep the former. Four credited sources: Aristotle (akrasia), Cialdini (whose six sales-influence methods — liking, authority, commitment/consistency, social proof, reciprocity, scarcity — value investors repurposed as reasons bad financial actions get taken), Kahneman and Tversky (availability, among much else).

**The frictionless-trading problem.** In 1989, acting on news meant putting down the paper, phoning a broker, placing an order — a lumbering sequence full of pauses where reason could intercept. The internet erased the circuit breakers: news in one window, execution in the other, no sitting down required. Reflection must therefore be *baked into the process*: **before any buy or sell order, tick through the list of 20** — the 18 biases and two akrasia forms, asked one by one ("is this the authority bias acting? reciprocity?…"). It doesn't take long and occasions just enough introspection to prevent the psychological class of mistake.

**The validity test.** The presence of a bias-shaped circumstance doesn't invalidate an idea — watching admired investors is a fine idea *source*. A judgment is rejected only when **born of** a bias: existing *just because* of it — the model doesn't recommend the investment and one proceeds anyway. Biases stack: Marshall's own three Preface errors decompose as Coca-Cola held past worth in 1998 (affinity for the icon + authority of a respected holder + consistency with his prior praise), Nike sold at P/B 3 in 2010 (anchoring on his low purchase price + authority of a famous seller + consensus of funds around him), and Mueller Industries unpurchased in 2011 despite deep familiarity and an obviously cyclical housing dip (intermixing inverted — just another industrial in a portfolio of them; miscontrast inverted — one of several good options at the time; peculiarity — a peer's suggestion, not his own). Errors of commission and omission both; the charm of the discipline is that its caution costs occasional upsides forgone while flagging disasters incomparably — for every triumph missed, a hundred disappointments avoided.

**The 18 cognitive biases** (each operates forward and, for most, inverted):

1. **Affinity** — liking something associated with the stock (charming CEO, product sizzle) drives the buy; inverted, disliking a red herring kills a sound idea.
2. **Reciprocity** — being treated well nudges toward buying and away from scrutiny (IR courtship of professionals); a perceived slight (no conference invite) deprioritizes a stock. Distinct from affinity: requires an actual action by the investee toward the investor.
3. **Anchoring** — benchmarking against an insignificant baseline: buying because the price fell from a high; selling because it rose past the purchase price. Only price-relative-to-worth matters — a stock up hugely but still below intrinsic value is likely to rise more.
4. **Authority** — following the admired; inverted, dismissing ideas from sources deemed beneath one.
5. **Availability** — overweighting what surfaces in memory with force: the recent, the vivid, the emotionally resonant (Kahneman–Tversky).
6. **Cleverness** — favoring ideas that exercise mental muscle and let one swim in one's own intelligence; inverted, distrusting the quickly understood — though many worthy ideas are truly basic.
7. **Incomprehensibility** — wanting an investment *more* the less it is understood, conflating unintelligibility with specialness and its promoter with expertise; inverted, hating plainly presented ideas for being insufficiently hard. Distinct from cleverness: cleverness needs genuine complexity, incomprehensibility only complicated *expression*.
8. **Consensus** — doing what everyone does; buys popularity at bid-up prices. Unpopularity is necessary-but-not-sufficient for a bargain.
9. **Peculiarity** — the contrarian's disease: wanting uniqueness for its own sake; inverted, rejecting whatever one didn't think of first.
10. **Intermixing** — favoring holdings that would stand out in one's *own* portfolio (the film limited-partnership next to the stocks); inverted, rejecting the undistinctive. Distinct from diversification, which fills a portfolio so not everything falls at once — intermixing picks different assets for kicks.
11. **Consistency** — acting in concert with prior actions; unwillingness to reverse a known position; buying more of a holding whose real flaws have surfaced. Distinct from perseverance, a virtue: holding fast through an inconsequential price drop is perseverance; holding through deteriorated fundamentals because one already owns it is consistency.
12. **Confirmation** — consistency without the precedent action; mere beliefs suffice. Repels ideas that contradict one's conception of a trend, industry, or market, and robs one of belief updates.
13. **Hope** — curating information so the positive stands out and the unflattering is suppressed. A virtue in sports and entrepreneurship; useless with stocks, which don't care about our belief in them.
14. **Lossophobia** — hope inverted: panic-driven stabs at capital preservation; the urge to sell into a price drop while fundamentals hold. Equating a price drop with a worth drop is never right. Its monument: the stop-loss order, which guarantees a loss and refuses the buying opportunity — better named a make-loss order.
15. **Scarcity** — wanting what seems in short supply (the oversubscribed IPO); inverted, distrusting the abundant and easy to access; on the sell side, delaying a deserved sale because the exit window seems lasting.
16. **Hotness** — the hot-hand fallacy: a streak of good outcomes implies magic on the next buy; inverted, a losing streak withholds warranted investment.
17. **Miscontrast** — choosing what's merely better than the current field rather than good absolutely; assumes the low caliber of today's opportunities will persist; ties up the capital that a truly great later idea needs — it strips the option value of cash. Surfaces when prices are high and bargains scarce.
18. **Windfallapathy** — carelessness born of sudden money (inheritance, business sale): frothy IPOs and chancy exotica, as if whittling the net worth back to its familiar level; playful at onset, best neutered early.

**The two forms of akrasia** (Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics*) — misaction without misjudgment: intelligent people, fully informed, acting against their own better judgment. (1) **Impetuosity** — acting on an urge without deliberation; feels like the instincts that serve elsewhere in life; beaten by recognizing the urge as fleeting while its consequences — permanent capital loss — are not. (2) **Weakness** — capitulating to passion *after* deliberation, doing the thing knowing it's foolish: Marshall's 2008 acquaintances, one a student of a behavioral-economics pioneer, selling fund interests at deep losses with no cash need and full knowledge prices would recover — failing on purpose. Weakness overlaps lossophobia; the inelegance is accepted because multiple lenses catch more errors in the making. Labels matter: naming the hazards is most of inoculating against them — feel one welling up, pause, regain footing.

## 9. Portfolios and selling (Ch. 17)

**Sizing: 10% of the portfolio or nothing.** If conviction doesn't support a tenth, buy none — not less. The rule is simultaneously a conviction test and an exposure cap: prefer not to exceed a tenth either, preserving cash for the next opportunity. Conviction matters because the price is almost certain to drop right after buying — the corollary of the near-certainty that one's purchase price won't be the low; rock-bottoms don't send invitations, so the astute investor counts on missing them.

**The two structural problems with selling.** (1) **Taxes.** The twin-portfolio illustration: two all-cash starts, both compounding at 15% pretax for 30 years, 30% long-term gains rate; portfolio one buys on day one and sells only at liquidation; portfolio two churns everything annually, repurchasing with after-tax proceeds. Portfolio one ends with **over twice** the cash — every annual tax payment whittled the base that the next year's 15% multiplied. Simplified, and the point stands: churn stunts compounding. Marshall draws a straight line from tax policy to investment policy — unrealized appreciation is untaxed, so everything else equal, holding wins; his quality focus, concentration, and indefinite horizon all trace back to it. (2) **Alternatives.** Understood, good, cheap companies don't go on sale daily; every sale mandates a hunt for the next one.

**The four valid sell reasons:** (1) **price flies past value** — EV/OI above 25 with no mitigating facts is hard to justify holding; (2) **the company turns out not to be good** — either the analysis was wrong (the entrant threat was stronger, the market wasn't growing) or the context changed (online-only sellers arrived; deregulation bit); the consistency bias fights this recognition, and selling here prevents a snag from ripping into both a realized loss and a missed redeployment; (3) **buyout** — perfunctory shareholder votes make it effectively involuntary; Marshall dislikes them (a pleasantly appreciating holding becomes a taxable event) but accepts profitable ones for lack of practical options; (4) **a clearly better opportunity needs the cash** — treated with extreme suspicion, because fresh ideas glow and the hope bias gets its prime shot; he reports never actually having done it.

**The invalid reasons:** **rebalancing** on the sell side is the anchoring bias in action (the buy-side version has some logic: a holding shrinking in portfolio weight without sales means its price fell — one could buy more cheaper); **memorializing success** — treating a sale as the finish line rests on the illusion that cash is more real than stock; unrealized gains are not endemically less concrete, and selling proves competence no better than intelligent holding; **agent compensation** — the 2-and-20's performance fee is levied on pretax returns, making managers structurally more enthusiastic about realizing gains than their tax-bearing limited partners should be.

**Passive cash generation.** Portfolios throw off cash without sales: buyouts, and dividends — whose growth against a fixed cost basis compounds invisibly to outsiders (Marshall's Nike: ~2% quoted yield at purchase and still ~2% a decade later at sale, but ~10% on his cost — the statistic "current dividend ÷ current price" hides it because *current* belongs only in the numerator for an owner).

**Concentration.** Never more than a dozen names, usually far fewer, on purpose: the more a portfolio resembles an index, the more it behaves like one, and it's hard to both resemble and outperform something (Ch. 20 adds the law of large numbers: each name is an experiment, and more experiments pull the average toward the expected — index — result). A bad focused portfolio can still lag; concentration is necessary-not-sufficient, and its promise comes from purposeful construction.

**Sequestered cash — what makes concentration survivable.** Keep several years of living expenses *outside* the equity portfolio, in government-insured bank accounts (stay under the insured limits — FDIC $250k, FSCS £75k, CDIC C$100k — across several banks if needed), in the **home currency** (post-Brexit sterling bought by dollar-spenders is a currency bet, not sequestered cash), never to be anything but cash or spent. Excluded: CDs/time deposits (early-withdrawal penalties defeat costless accessibility) and cash-like funds (a 2008 acquaintance found withdrawals from a commercial-paper fund frozen exactly when needed). Inflation erodes it — accepted; what's bought is the guarantee of availability, which converts a halved portfolio price (Marshall's own 2008) from a crisis into a non-event: all expenses covered, no basis for panic, no forced sale at the bottom. This is the mechanism that lets one actually *hold* through what concentration guarantees will come.

## 10. Endurance, idea generation, and the valid differences (Ch. 18–20)

**Ethics as an endurance problem (Ch. 18).** The framing is deliberately practical, not moralist: long-term after-tax outperformance requires holding good companies a long time, which requires a portfolio *consistent* with one's moral posture — not praiseworthy, consistent — because a sudden awakening of principles forces uneconomic sales, and if it strikes when prices are down, financial health pays for the epiphany. Four postures observed in smart people: **amorality** (investing sits outside ethical consideration; the imperative is growing wealth), **moral failure abstention** (a list of don'ts — no tobacco, no handguns), **moral success affirmation** (a list of dos — only renewables etc.), **moral failure activism** (buy the offender to force change from inside, exploiting shareholders' agenda-setting power). No advocacy among them — the advice is only to pick one early, since ethical views change less over a lifetime than most other facets of an investor's makeup, and reckoning with them is either early and painless or later and less so.

**Seven idea sources (Ch. 19)** — inspirations only; none credentials an idea for preferential treatment:

1. **Bad news.** Headlines amplify extremes; human reaction overshoots; professionals overshoot too (a fund dumps an embarrassing-but-fine name before the quarterly holdings snapshot). Three grades, each worked: *warranted but overdone* — Anheuser-Busch 2005, written off in the papers as beer losing to vodka–Red Bull; the analysis showed a thriving core, a ballpark outing showed people drinking a lot of beer, and $45 stock was bought — acquired at $70 in November 2008, ~15% annualized with dividends (the buyout unsought and disliked, but taxes on gains are the preferred kind of financial pain). *Fully justified* — Volkswagen 2015: an organized effort to skirt emissions rules, and a company whose ROCE was underwhelming before the scandal — little opportunity for the decades-long holder. *Totally unjustified* — Twitter's TWTR ticker announcement sent bankrupt Tweeter Home Entertainment's TWTRQ on a surge-and-crash. Sensationalism widens the price-value gap the investor exists to bridge; good investing has no better friend than bad journalism.
2. **Spin-offs.** New shares arrive as a dividend to the parent's holders and start trading; institutional holders often must sell automatically (below-minimum market cap, mandate mismatch) — forced selling that depresses prices of businesses that may be understood and good.
3. **Regulatory filings (13Fs).** US managers over $100m AUM file quarterly long-position lists, 45 days after quarter-end (mid-February/May/August/November at sec.gov); comparing periods reveals what talented professionals bought. **Seven limitations:** (i) one must know who's worth following, and track records of private and in-house managers are invisible — fame is not a proxy for performance; (ii) longs only — the visible half of a long/short pair is not the bet; (iii) no purchase prices (the quarter's low bounds them from below, nothing more); (iv) the 45-day lag — positions may already be gone or rebuilt; (v) the filing itself moves prices as copycats pile in, erasing the inexpensiveness; (vi) a debut position may be a spin-off receipt, possibly already being sold — the opposite of a signal; (vii) the authority bias invites unthinking mimicry — masters make mistakes. The counter-bias runs the other way too: peculiarity makes 13F-mining feel parroty — dirty, even — which the restaurateur analogy dissolves: a free, accurate quarterly letter describing what the country's best restaurant just did would never be thrown out unread, and reading imposes no obligation to buy the same fryer. A disclosure is not a directive; treat a debut as an invitation to analyze from scratch.
4. **Reorganizations.** Mergers, capital-structure overhauls, division sales — complications repel most buyers, thinning the pool and lowering the price for whoever will sort them out.
5. **Small caps** (roughly < $2bn). Institutions are barred by charter or defeated by arithmetic — a $50bn fund buying 10% of a $500m company that then doubles moves its own needle a tenth of a percent — leaving prices unattended. Adjacent reality: small-cap investing shades toward involvement, both because it's *necessary* (thin institutional oversight lets managements stray) and because it's *possible* (presidents answer e-mail).
6. **Screeners.** Marshall's least favorite: valuation-first is the cart before the horse for someone whose gates run understand → good → cheap, and screens surface outlying financial situations unfit for lifetime holding. Others use them well.
7. **Serendipity.** Mental preparedness to receive tips from everyday life — chance-driven but favoring the open mind. Clas Ohlson entered the funnel because every Stockholm store visit showed crowds buying (the disconfirmation hunt around Sweden found one empty store — after closing time); the stock never got cheap enough, which is the system working. Tesco exited the funnel when the California Fresh & Easy visit found high quality, fair prices, attentive staff — and one customer; the analysis stopped, the price later plunged, the chain closed. Serendipity turns ordinary environments into evidence — logos on shoes, passengers on planes, the brand on the broken escalator — a spigot the investor controls rather than a life on alert.

**Then forget the provenance.** Once an idea enters the model, forgetting whether it came from a spin-off, a hero's 13F, or a price plunge keeps a whole raft of biases at bay — raw material plus the clear mind to process it.

**Eight valid differences among value investors (Ch. 20)** — the price/value distinction is the *only* requisite principle; beyond it: asset class (listed equities return best, but some squeeze performance from bonds or property); holding period (short-horizon holders need *catalysts* — reasons a depressed price could soar, like surprise results or an executive exit; the never-selling holder doesn't give them a thought); activism vs inactivism (activism works only with extraordinary gravitas and tenacity — not for dabblers); diversification vs concentration (thresholds scale with capital; small-cap strategies can force diversification via share scarcity); quality vs cheap-garbage (an equally convinced school buys badness cheaply enough and some excel; Marshall's quality focus is the joint product of inactivism, concentration, and the indefinite tax-driven horizon — what he buys must be good because he won't fix it, holds few, and keeps it forever); leverage; complexity (some seek convoluted instruments precisely because complication thins buy-side competition); shorting. **Two invite suspicion:** shorting — theoretically appealing, practically thorny (scarce borrow, fees, squeezes), and Marshall knows of no *principals* who both rely on it and outperform long-term — it's the hallmark of 2-and-20 complexity, a ticket out of the land of 1%; and leverage — milkable most of the time, but one margin call fouls years, and for long horizons something that doesn't work sometimes is indistinguishable from something that doesn't work at all.

## 11. Preservation and maintenance (Ch. 21)

Losing money is worse than not losing money, and the math is the argument: a realized −50% year needs +100% the next just to reach zero — investing scores misses negatively, unlike soccer, where a missed shot leaves the score alone and the next ball is a fresh start. That arithmetic is why capital preservation ranks first and why value investors act with restraint; the majority that believes in the risk-return trade-off subordinates preservation as a step *toward* outperformance, and the majority of actively managed equity funds trails basic indexes — connected facts. Two practices preserve the model itself: **keep the bottom layer current** (accounting standards and disclosure regimes will change; a new OI definition would force ROCE to adapt; killed RPT disclosure would trim the SF indicators to three — the more time since publication, the more vigilance) and **think in percents** (a 20% realized loss isn't fine because it was only $1,000; a $1m gain isn't impressive at 2% annualized; percent-thinking builds habits that scale from little capital to much without new tricks). The closing register, kept for doctrine: value investing pays, engages one with the world (the serendipity spigot), is at root truth-seeking, rewards the long view, and remains practicable long after careers requiring meetings and flights get hard; above subsistence, money buys not the great joys but protection from their loss — the freedom to drop everything for what matters, fearlessly, at times of one's choosing.

**Why ownership, not pledges (R8, answered through Kenneth's lens).** The book's ideal insider holds shares bought with his own cash that make up most of his wealth, and it concedes that this is rarely knowable (Ch. 13). What can be known is how much of the company management owns, and how that holding compares with what management is paid; R8 judges alignment on exactly that, at the memo price. A pledge is a private borrowing against shares the insider still owns: their gain and loss stay his, so the shares count in full and the pledge is recorded as a fact. Sales likewise count only through the holding they leave, since the book itself finds ordinary sales inconclusive (Ch. 13). Kenneth's view stands as dissent (D35): a pledge moves part of the downside to the lender and can make a forced seller of the person running the company (Ch. 20), and the book makes an exception for several insiders selling most of their shares at once.
